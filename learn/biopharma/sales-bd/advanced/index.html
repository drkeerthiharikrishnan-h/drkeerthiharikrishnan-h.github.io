    Learn

    Start with the question and the label, not the model

    The FDA’s January 2025 draft guidance on AI credibility starts with the question of interest, then the context of use (COU), meaning the exact role the model’s output plays in a decision[1]. Only then does it ask about model risk, which it builds from two parts: how much the output influences the decision, and how bad the consequence of a wrong decision would be[1]. The draft is non-binding and leaves drug discovery out of its scope[1]. A discovery-only potency model is a research purchase; a tool whose output supports a regulatory decision about safety, effectiveness or quality falls inside the credibility frame[1].

    Next, ask what the label is. A label is the answer the model learns to reproduce. For an activity model, the label is often “active” or “inactive”, cut from a measured IC50 (the concentration that halves an enzyme’s activity). ChEMBL expresses potency as pChEMBL, minus log10 of the molar value, so 1 nM is 9 and 1 µM is 6[2]. For a competitive inhibitor the observed IC50 rises with substrate concentration, so a label from one assay version does not carry over to another run under other conditions[3]. “Active if pIC50 is at least 6 in assay record ASY-KIN-07 version 3” is a label. “Active” on its own is not.

    Data readiness then decides whether the task is feasible at all. Positive examples are scarce at every stage of the funnel. One discovery project may screen 200,000 to over 1,000,000 compounds and end with one or two candidates[4]. In a study of 9,704 programmes from 2011 to 2020, 7.9% of those entering Phase I reached approval[5]. A model trained to predict approval from past programmes would see about 8 positives per 100 examples. The MoleculeNet authors found that learned molecular representations still struggle when data are scarce and classes are highly imbalanced[6].

    For a seller these are the first scoping questions. Who owns the decision? Which record defines the label, in which version? How many positives exist today? Expect a different owner for each use: discovery chemistry for an activity model on small molecules, protein science for structure work on antibodies and recombinant proteins, clinical operations, medical writing or regulatory affairs for search over protocols and study reports, pharmacovigilance for safety cases. The building blocks below are shared; the owner, the data and the stakes are not.

    Embeddings: turning text, molecules and proteins into vectors

    An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that stands for a piece of content, built so that similar content gets nearby vectors. Sentence-BERT (SBERT) produces sentence embeddings that are compared by cosine similarity, a measure of the angle between two vectors[7]. Finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences takes about 65 hours when a BERT model reads every pair, and about 5 seconds with embeddings[7]. The MTEB benchmark covered 8 task types, 58 datasets and 112 languages and found no single method dominant[8]. A leaderboard rank says little about protocols or batch records.

    Molecules have their own embeddings. Extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP) break a molecule into substructures grown outward from each heavy atom, then hash them into a fixed-length binary fingerprint used for similarity search and activity prediction[6]. MoleculeNet uses ECFP4 from the open-source RDKit library; a similarity value means little unless the fingerprint type is named[6].

    Proteins have them too. ESM-2 is a protein language model trained on sequences. As the model grows to 15 billion parameters, an atomic-resolution picture of structure emerges in its learned representations, and ESMFold reads a 3D structure out of them[9]. Those representations are protein embeddings, usable to cluster sequences before any structure exists.

    Documents must be cut into chunks before they are embedded. The original retrieval-augmented generation paper split each Wikipedia article into disjoint 100-word chunks, 21 million in all, and embedded each one[10]. A clinical study report follows one core structure accepted in all ICH regions[11], so chunks can follow its numbered sections. A trial master file must support document identification, version history, search and retrieval[12]. Each chunk should carry its document id, version, status, section, page and study, so retrieval can filter before it ranks and an answer can point back to its source.

    Metadata filters work best on controlled codes, not free text. Useful codes include the NCT number of a registered trial, “NCT” followed by 8 digits[13]; the UniProt accession of a protein, 6 or 10 characters[14]; and a MedDRA term for an adverse event, from a five-level hierarchy whose guide is versioned (version 29.0 for the guide cited here)[15]. A study id typed as “ABC 301” instead of the controlled form drops out of a study filter silently, as the messy chunk table below shows[data].

    Retrieval: lexical, dense and hybrid

    Retrieval finds the chunks or records most likely to answer a query. Lexical retrieval such as BM25 scores documents by the query’s own words. It treats text as a bag of words, so it cannot find a passage that says the same thing in other words, the “lexical gap”[16]. Dense retrieval returns the nearest vectors, so “renal impairment” can match “reduced kidney function”. On the BEIR benchmark of 18 datasets, BM25 was a robust baseline; dense models were cheaper than re-rankers but often underperformed on new domains[16]. Some dense models strong in their training domain fell below BM25 on many unseen datasets[16].

    A hybrid design runs both and merges the two ranked lists. The lexical side still matches exact strings such as a lot number, an NCT number or a gene symbol, which dense vectors can blur; the dense side catches paraphrase[16]. Which mix works is for the evaluation to show on the buyer’s documents.

    A vector database stores embeddings and answers nearest-neighbour queries fast. Most use approximate search. HNSW, for example, builds a layered graph of nearby points and searches it from the top layer down[17]. The RAG paper’s index used FAISS with this HNSW approximation over its 21 million chunks[10]. Approximate search can miss a true neighbour, so recall is measured on the customer’s corpus, not assumed.

    Regulated retrieval adds a locator to every result: document, version, section and page, or table, row and column for a structured record. Without it a reviewer cannot confirm the answer, and the trial master file rules on version history and retrieval apply to whatever the tool cites[12].

    Reranking and grounded generation

    First-stage retrieval is fast but rough. A reranker rereads each candidate together with the query and reorders the short list. A cross-encoder such as the BERT reranker of Nogueira and Cho scores each query-passage pair jointly; it reached the top of the MS MARCO passage leaderboard at the time with a 27% relative gain in MRR@10[18]. The price is compute: that joint reading is what made the 10,000-sentence search take about 65 hours[7]. So the stack reranks only a short candidate list. On BEIR, re-ranking and late-interaction models gave the best average zero-shot results, at a high computational cost[16]. A buyer should see the quality gain and the extra latency per query.

    Reranking fixes the top of the list, the part a language model actually reads. If the right record sits at rank 7 and the answer is written from the top 5, the answer is wrong although retrieval “found” it.

    Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) passes the top passages to a language model that writes the answer. Lewis and colleagues named the design: a trained model’s parametric memory combined with a non-parametric memory, a dense vector index read by a retriever[10]. Their motivation fits regulated work: models that hold facts only in their parameters struggle to give provenance and to update what they know[10]. Grounding means every claim is supported by a retrieved passage; Ragas scores separately whether retrieval found relevant passages and whether the answer stays faithful to them[19].

    Abstention is the other half. When no record answers the question, the correct output is “no record found” with the filters applied; a plausible value in its place is invented. The test set should include questions whose right answer is “no record”.

    Model predictions are not measurements

    A QSAR model (quantitative structure-activity relationship) predicts activity, or an ADMET property (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, toxicity), from structure. The OECD principles for QSAR models used for regulatory purposes ask for a defined endpoint, an unambiguous algorithm, a defined domain of applicability, measures of fit, robustness and predictivity, and a mechanistic interpretation if possible[20]. The applicability domain is the chemical space where the model can be trusted. It exists because such models are simplifications that predict reliably only for certain structures, properties and mechanisms[20]. A prediction for a compound unlike anything in training is an extrapolation and should be flagged as such.

    AlphaFold and ESMFold predict a protein’s 3D structure from sequence and report their own confidence. pLDDT is a per-residue score from 0 to 100: 90 and above is very high, 70 to under 90 confident, 50 to under 70 low, and under 50 very low, often a disordered region[21]. PAE (predicted aligned error) is a matrix in angstroms. Its value at (x, y) is the expected position error at residue x when the predicted and true structures are aligned on residue y[22]. High PAE between two domains means their relative placement is unreliable, even when each domain on its own looks confident[22].

    One summary number can mislead. For UniProt accession P00533 the AlphaFold Database API returns model AF-P00533-F1 with a mean pLDDT of 75.94 and residue fractions of 0.228 very low, 0.065 low, 0.233 confident and 0.474 very high[23]. UniProt lists that accession as the EGFR gene product, 1,210 residues long[24]. The mean sits in the “confident” band, yet 0.228 × 1,210 is about 276 residues of very low confidence. A claim of a “high-confidence structure” needs the per-residue picture and the PAE, not the mean.

    Docking and free-energy methods are predictions too. Docking places a small molecule in a protein pocket and scores the pose. Asked whether docking is the right tool to estimate binding affinity, Pantsar and Poso answer no, as several comprehensive analyses had concluded[25]. Free-energy perturbation (FEP) calculations estimate the relative binding potency of related compounds. An error of 1 kcal/mol can mean a wrong prediction of up to a 5-fold difference in binding affinity[26]. Ross and colleagues at Schrödinger, the developer of the software tested, report a median root-mean-square error of 1.08 kcal/mol across 1,237 ligands, estimated retrospectively, and say reported errors are typically higher in prospective use[27]. It is a developer-published result, for a buyer to test on its own series. Neither a docking score nor an FEP estimate is a measured IC50 or KD, and a careful team never stores one in a column meant for measurements[25, 27].

    Splits, leakage and the join to public records

    A test score is only as honest as the split behind it. A random split puts close analogues of training compounds into the test set, which flatters the model. The MoleculeNet authors note that random splitting is often not correct for chemical data, and that a scaffold split, which groups molecules by their two-dimensional core framework, is a harder test[6]. Sheridan showed that a time split, training on older compounds and testing on newer ones, gives an estimate closer to real prospective use than a random split, which is too optimistic[28]. The FDA draft asks that test data be independent of development data[1]. The EMA reflection paper evaluates the final model once on hold-out test data, and strongly encourages an early train-test split, before any normalisation[29].

    The example table shows the size of the effect. The same model scores an AUROC of 0.91 on a random split, 0.79 on a scaffold split and 0.76 on a time split, and a nearest-neighbour baseline scores 0.70 on the scaffold split[data]. Quote the scaffold or time number, next to the baseline.

    Leakage is any path by which test information reaches training. In chemistry the commonest is one molecule under two ids, such as a new salt form registered as a new compound. The InChIKey catches it. It is a fixed 27-character hashed signature of a structure; the first 14-character block encodes the skeleton and the second block the remaining layers such as stereochemistry and isotopes[30]. Deduplicate on the key, or on its first block to group stereoisomers, before splitting. In the messy table 37 of 1,000 test compounds share an InChIKey with training, and the scaffold-split AUROC rises from 0.79 to 0.86[data].

    The same key joins internal records to public data. PubChem’s PUG-REST service accepts an InChIKey as input and returns compound records and properties[31]. ChEMBL’s web services return molecule, activity, assay and target records by ChEMBL id[32]. A careful join runs registry compound to InChIKey to PubChem CID and ChEMBL id, then checks the internal label against public activities for the same target. For proteins the key is the UniProt accession. UniProt’s REST API and ID Mapping tool convert gene names and other identifiers[24], and the AlphaFold Database serves the predicted model for an accession as AF-accession-F1[23]. Open Targets scores target-disease associations from 20 evidence sources (in its 2021 description)[33]. ClinicalTrials.gov returns one study at /api/v2/studies/ followed by its NCT number[34], which is how a protocol chunk can be checked against the public registration.

    Knowledge graphs: identifiers and ontology versions are the product

    A knowledge graph stores facts as nodes (compounds, genes, diseases) and edges (the relations between them). Hetionet, a public example, has 47,031 nodes of 11 types and 2,250,197 relationships of 24 types, built from 29 public resources[35]. Its builders converted every identifier into the vocabulary of the matching node type before adding relationships. They mapped on gene symbols only when there was no other option, because symbols can be ambiguous[35]. So nodes should carry the identifiers taught earlier: a UniProt accession for a protein, an InChIKey for a compound, a MedDRA term for an adverse event, an OMOP concept id for a coded clinical record[14, 30, 15, 36]. Edges need provenance too. Hetionet’s recommended formats carry the source, licence and confidence score on nodes and edges[35].

    Disease terms come from ontologies: controlled lists of terms, each with an id and defined relations to other terms. The EMBL-EBI Ontology Lookup Service (OLS) is a free search engine across them[37]. Ontologies change. The OBO Foundry asks each ontology to issue dated releases that are never altered, so users can state exactly which version encoded their data[38]. Terms are retired and replaced. In the MONDO disease ontology release of 2026-10-06, MONDO:0000126 is an obsolete gastric cancer term, replaced by MONDO:0001056[39]. An edge still keyed on the old id no longer joins with data coded to the new one, although both labels read “gastric cancer”[data].

    So a graph has a version, made of the releases of its sources. A graph answer needs the same locator as a retrieval answer: the edge, its evidence record and the ontology release. For a seller the scoping questions are: which vocabulary keys each node type, in which release; who maps free text to terms; and what happens to the graph when a source issues a new release.

    Reading the numbers: retrieval metrics, prediction metrics, calibration

    Retrieval metrics ask where the right record landed. Recall@k is the share of queries whose correct record appears in the top k. MRR (mean reciprocal rank) averages 1 divided by the rank of the first correct result, so rank 1 scores 1, rank 2 scores 0.5 and a miss scores 0; Nogueira and Cho report it as MRR@10[18]. Plain precision and recall ignore rank, MRR handles only right-or-wrong relevance, and nDCG@k (normalised discounted cumulative gain) also handles graded relevance, which is why BEIR reports nDCG@10[16].

    AUROC is the area under the curve of true-positive rate against false-positive rate. On imbalanced data the precision-recall plot changes with class balance while the ROC plot does not, and Saito and Rehmsmeier conclude the precision-recall plot is the more informative one when positives are rare[40]. The no-skill baseline for PR-AUC is the share of positives. With 50 actives among 1,000 test compounds a random ranking scores about 0.05, so a PR-AUC of 0.22 is 4.4 times that baseline[data]. MoleculeNet scores its large, imbalanced screening sets PCBA and MUV with PRC-AUC[6].

    Calibration asks whether a predicted probability means what it says: of the compounds given 0.8, about 80% should turn out active. Guo and colleagues found modern neural networks poorly calibrated and showed that temperature scaling, a one-parameter correction, works on most datasets they tested[41]. It matters whenever a score sets a quantity, such as how many compounds to make.

    Reference-free metrics such as those in Ragas need no human labels[19], so they suit development but not acceptance. The FDA, Health Canada and MHRA good machine learning practice principles for medical devices call for multidisciplinary expertise across the life cycle[42]. For AI in drug development, EMA and FDA published ten joint guiding principles of good AI practice in January 2026. They are high-level principles meant to inform future guidance and standards, and they too name multidisciplinary expertise and a clear context of use[43]. In practice a subject-matter expert grades a sample of frozen outputs against the source.

    GxP, the buying process and a credible evaluation plan

    When an AI output feeds a regulated decision, the FDA draft lays out seven steps: question, context of use, model risk, credibility plan, execution, documentation of results and deviations, and a decision on adequacy for the context of use[1]. The EMA reflection paper, adopted in September 2024, takes a risk-based view. For a pivotal trial it expects the pre-processing pipeline and models to be frozen and documented before unblinding, and it does not accept incremental learning there[29].

    In this stack “the model” is several versioned parts: the embedding model, the index, the reranker, the language model, the prompt, and dictionaries such as MedDRA. EU GMP Annex 11 says changes to a computerised system are made only in a controlled way under a defined procedure, and that the extent of validation follows a documented risk assessment[44]. 21 CFR 11.10(e) requires secure, time-stamped audit trails of who created, changed or deleted an electronic record, without hiding earlier entries[45]. FDA’s 2003 scope guidance applies enforcement discretion to some Part 11 audit-trail requirements, while predicate rules still apply[46]. Re-embedding an index or moving to a new MedDRA version is a change request with tests, not a silent update. ICH Q9(R1) scales the formality and documentation to the level of risk[47].

    The buying committee is the one met at beginner level. What is new here is validation per layer. Annex 11 expects validation documents across the life cycle, justified by risk, together with user requirements, evidence that the supplier works under a suitable quality system, and evidence of test methods and scenarios[44]. ICH E6(R3) bases the validation of a trial computerised system on a risk assessment of its intended use, the importance of the data and its effect on participants and results[48]. So the vendor’s pack lists each layer (embedding model, index, reranker, language model, prompt) with its version, its tests, the test evidence and the change that would reopen them. In clinical trials a sponsor may rely on that documentation after assessing it, for example through an audit[49].

    Proof is a measured result on the buyer’s data, with a named baseline and a frozen configuration; a press release, blog or benchmark on another domain is signal. The beginner module read the ESMFold speed claim for its baseline. The new step here is to count the whole pipeline. The preprint’s 6x at 384 residues, and its up to about 60x on shorter sequences, compare ESMFold with a single AlphaFold2 model on the inference step alone[9]. AlphaFold also needs a search for related sequences. That search can take over 10 minutes with high-sensitivity protocols, or under 1 minute with reduced sensitivity[9]. So the end-to-end gain depends on which search the buyer runs, as Try it 4 shows. No figure in the paper is a promise about a customer’s sequences.

    A credible evaluation section states, per layer: the question and context of use; the versioned corpus or compound set; the label and who set it; a scaffold or time split and a simple baseline; metrics with prevalence (recall@k and MRR, or PR-AUC with its baseline and a calibration check); the “no record” questions; who grades; and which changes trigger change control. It promises no outcomes.

    Promotional content: MLR review is the gate, whoever drafts

    A tool that drafts promotional material is a different sale from one that answers medical questions. Medical science liaisons do scientific exchange, not promotion[50]. Promotional pieces pass the company’s own review before use; vendors call it MLR review (medical, legal and regulatory)[51]. The rules that review checks against are public. In Europe, the EFPIA Code, a self-regulatory industry code, says promotion must be consistent with the summary of product characteristics (SmPC), the approved product information. It must not promote a medicine before its marketing authorisation or outside its approved indications[52]. Claims must be capable of substantiation, and published studies must be clearly referenced[52]. A medical doctor or, where appropriate, a pharmacist approves each piece before release and certifies that they examined its final form[52]. In the US, a prescription drug advertisement must give a true statement of side effects, contraindications and effectiveness. It is misleading if it suggests a benefit, or a wider use, that the approved labeling does not allow[53]. For a drug approved under a new drug application (NDA), the company sends each promotional piece to the FDA when it is first used, with Form FDA 2253 and the current labeling[54].

    So an AI draft goes through the same review as a human draft, and a named person still signs the final form[52]. The buyer will ask about the review record: which version was reviewed, which label section or reference supports each claim, who approved it and when. A tool that links every claim to its support shortens that work. A tool that writes unsupported claims adds to it. One software vendor launched an agentic MLR product in June 2026 that checks promotional and medical materials against approved labels[51]. That is a launch: a signal, not yet shown in production. Scoping questions: is the tool drafting promotion or medical information, for which markets, and who signs today?

    Try it

    1. Using the good retrieval log, compute recall@1, recall@3, recall@5 and MRR over the six queries. Which single query would a reranker most need to fix, and which one can no reranker fix?[data]
      AnswerRanks are 1, 1, 2, not in top 5, 3 and 1[data]. Recall@1 = 3 / 6 = 50%. Recall@3 = 5 / 6 = 83%. Recall@5 = 5 / 6 = 83%. MRR = (1 + 1 + 0.5 + 0 + 0.333 + 1) / 6 = 3.833 / 6 = 0.64. A reranker reorders only what retrieval returned, so Q-03 (rank 2) and Q-05 (rank 3) are the candidates to move up; Q-05 gains most, from 0.333 to 1. Q-04 never reached the top 5, so no reranker can fix it: the first stage must find it, by a better filter, a lexical match on the lot id, or a larger candidate list. MRR is the metric Nogueira and Cho report for reranking, at cut-off 10[18].
    2. In the good model summary, compute the PR-AUC baseline and the lift over baseline for RUN-01 to RUN-05. Which run would you quote in a proposal, and what would you print next to it?[data]
      AnswerBaseline = actives / test compounds[data]. RUN-01: 50 / 1000 = 0.05, lift 0.48 / 0.05 = 9.6. RUN-02: 0.05, lift 0.22 / 0.05 = 4.4. RUN-03: 27 / 900 = 0.03, lift 0.15 / 0.03 = 5.0. RUN-04 (baseline model): 0.05, lift 0.12 / 0.05 = 2.4. RUN-05: 9 / 120 = 0.075, lift 0.30 / 0.075 = 4.0. Raw PR-AUC values cannot be compared across runs with different prevalence, because PR curves change with class balance[40]. Quote RUN-02 or RUN-03, not RUN-01: random splits are optimistic for chemistry[6, 28]. Print beside it the split, the prevalence, the nearest-neighbour baseline (AUROC 0.79 against 0.70, PR-AUC 0.22 against 0.12) and the prospective result in RUN-05.
    3. In the messy model summary, find the two rows where the numbers look better than the clean run for a reason that has nothing to do with the model. Quantify each.[data]
      AnswerRow 2: 37 of 1,000 test compounds share an InChIKey with training, 3.7% of the test set, and AUROC rises from 0.79 to 0.86 and PR-AUC from 0.22 to 0.35[data]. The fix is to deduplicate on the InChIKey, whose first 14-character block encodes the skeleton, before splitting[30]. Row 5: the training cut-off of 2024-09-30 falls 91 days after the freeze of 2024-07-01 (30 days left in July, 31 in August, 30 in September), so “prospective” compounds can sit in training; AUROC goes from 0.74 to 0.83. The EMA reflection paper evaluates the final model once on hold-out test data, and for a pivotal trial expects the models frozen and documented before unblinding[29].
    4. A vendor slide says “ESMFold: 60x faster than AlphaFold2”. The buyer wants 1,000 structures for proteins of about 384 residues. Use the paper’s own figures to estimate both inference times, then add AlphaFold’s alignment search, once with a high-sensitivity search and once with a fast one. What do you tell the buyer?[9]
      AnswerAt 384 residues ESMFold takes 14.2 s, 6x faster than one AlphaFold2 model, so one AlphaFold2 model takes about 14.2 × 6 = 85.2 s[9]. For 1,000 proteins: ESMFold 14,200 s, about 3.9 h; AlphaFold2 inference 85,200 s, about 23.7 h. With high-sensitivity alignment search (over 10 minutes per protein), add more than 1,000 × 600 s = 600,000 s, about 167 h; end to end the gain is then about (85.2 + 600) / 14.2 = 48x or more. With a fast search of under 1 minute it is at most about (85.2 + 60) / 14.2 = 10x. The 60x itself applies to short sequences and the inference step alone, and the 6x compares against a single AlphaFold2 model[9]. Tell the buyer the measured speed depends on sequence length, hardware and whether the search step is counted, and offer to time a sample of their sequences. Speed says nothing about accuracy; check pLDDT and PAE on the same set[21, 22].
    5. Apply the filter “status effective or final, study STUDY-ABC-301” to the good chunk index, then to the messy one. What comes back, and what should the system answer to “What is the schedule of assessments in PRT-ABC-301?” in each case?[data]
      AnswerGood index: CH-0001, CH-0002 and CH-0006, all version 3.0 with a section and page; the answer comes from CH-0006, section 9.1, page 52[data]. Messy index: CH-0001 (no locator) and CH-0008 (version 2.0 wrongly marked effective); CH-0006, the schedule of assessments chunk, drops out because its study id reads “ABC 301”. The system then abstains wrongly, or answers from the superseded version. Absence from an index is not absence from the document: before a “no record” answer counts as correct, check that the effective version was fully indexed. The 3,900-token CSR chunk is about 9.5 times the 412-token protocol chunk (3900 / 412), so its locator spans sections 12.2 to 14.3 and cannot point to one place. Version history and retrieval are trial master file requirements[12].
    6. A pitch says “AlphaFold gives a high-confidence model of the buyer’s target, UniProt P00533”. Using the database figures, how many residues fall in each pLDDT band, and how would you rewrite the claim?[23, 24]
      AnswerLength 1,210 residues[24]. Very low: 0.228 × 1,210 = 276. Low: 0.065 × 1,210 = 79. Confident: 0.233 × 1,210 = 282. Very high: 0.474 × 1,210 = 574 (rounding gives 1,211; the fractions are rounded)[23]. Confident or better: 0.707 × 1,210, about 855 residues. The mean of 75.94 sits in the 70 to under 90 band, but over a fifth of the chain is under 50, often disorder[21]. Rewrite: “AF-P00533-F1 has about 71% of residues at confident or very high pLDDT and 23% very low; domain placement must be checked in the PAE before using the model for design”[22].
    7. Join the has_indication edges to the tested_in_disease_model edges on the disease term id, first in the good knowledge-graph table, then in the messy one. Which project and target pairs come back, and how would you restore the missing ones?[data]
      AnswerGood table: KG-E-0001 (PRJ-0012) meets KG-E-0002 (TGT-0007) on MONDO:0001056, and KG-E-0003 (PRJ-0019) meets KG-E-0004 (TGT-0011) on MONDO:0018876, so 2 pairs. KG-E-0005 joins nothing, because no project has follicular lymphoma as its indication[data]. Messy table: 0 pairs. KG-E-0001 uses MONDO:0000126, KG-E-0003 has no id and KG-E-0004 uses a DOID id. To restore them: MONDO:0000126 is obsolete and replaced by MONDO:0001056 in the 2026-10-06 release; MONDO:0018876 lists DOID:0050746 as a cross-reference; and it lists “MCL” as a synonym, but a curator should confirm that mapping, because an abbreviation can mean other things[39]. That gives 2 pairs again. Record the release on every edge, so the next release can be checked the same way[38]. KG-E-0005 also needs its evidence record before anyone relies on it[35].
    8. An AI tool drafts a promotional email for an EU launch. One sentence says the medicine “also helps patients with” a condition that is not in the SmPC, and cites no study. What does the review do with it, and what record should the tool leave?
      AnswerThe sentence comes out. Promotion must be consistent with the SmPC and must not promote outside the approved indications; claims must be capable of substantiation, and published studies must be clearly referenced[52]. Who drafted it does not change this: the medical doctor or pharmacist who approves the piece certifies its final form[52]. The tool should leave a record of the draft version, each claim linked to its SmPC section or reference, the reviewer’s decision and the date. For the US, the same check runs against the approved labeling, and for a drug approved under an NDA the piece goes to the FDA at first use with Form FDA 2253[53, 54].

A careful team: a) deletes the edge b) matches on the label text instead c) follows the replacement term under change control and records the release d) keeps the old id, since the label still reads the same
