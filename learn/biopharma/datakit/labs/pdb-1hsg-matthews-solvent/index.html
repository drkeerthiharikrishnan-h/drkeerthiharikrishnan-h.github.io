1. The question

How tightly is the protein packed in the 1HSG crystal? What are its Matthews coefficient and solvent content, computed from the unit cell and the mass of the protein chains?

The two numbers tie together the cell, the symmetry and the contents of a crystal entry. Recomputing them is a quick consistency check on a structure file, and it shows how much of a protein crystal is solvent and not protein.

From: Field exptl_crystal of the entry record (density_Matthews and density_percent_sol)[1]

6. What goes wrong in practice

  • Z counts chains, not copies of the complex. The Z on the CRYST1 line is 8. The record's definition says it is the number of polymer chains in the unit cell. The file lists two chains and four symmetry operators, and 2 times 4 is 8. Use the mass of one chain with this Z, not the mass of the dimer. [4]
  • The definition read literally doubles the answer. The field definition speaks of the volume of the asymmetric unit over the mass of a monomer. In 1HSG the asymmetric unit holds two chains, so that reading gives 5.61 and not 2.80. Divide a volume by all the protein mass inside that same volume. [4] Seen in this lab's data; the notebook shows it.
  • The mass is in kilodaltons. The entity record gives the formula mass as 10.804, in kilodaltons. The coefficient is in cubic angstroms per dalton. Forget the factor of 1,000 and the coefficient comes out as 2,803. [5]
  • The second decimal of the solvent content does not reproduce. The PDB file prints the two values as 2.80 and 56.10. The notebook gets 2.803 and 56.12. Swapping the rounded constant 1.23 for the 1.229 it is derived from moves the solvent content to 56.15, and starting from the rounded 2.80 gives 56.07. The entry does not say which mass or constant was used. [4] Seen in this lab's data; the notebook shows it.
  • Solvent content is an estimate, not a measurement. The formula rests on an assumed partial specific volume of 0.74 cubic centimetres per gram for the molecule. Treat the percentage as a calculated estimate. [4]
  • Split a PDB line on spaces and the fields move. The space group on the CRYST1 line is written "P 21 21 2", with spaces inside it. Splitting the line on spaces gives twelve pieces, four of them from the space group, so Z has no fixed place in the list. Cut PDB-format lines by column position. Seen in this lab's data; the notebook shows it.

7. The same job on each platform

  • Open source executed here The lab's notebook uses only the Python standard library. It downloads the 1HSG structure file and the entity record (chain mass), reads the unit cell and the protein chains, and computes the Matthews coefficient and the solvent content. It then checks both against the values in the RCSB PDB entry. This site's check runs it in a fresh Python process.[6]
  • AWS documented, not executed herevendor-published Amazon S3 is an object storage service that stores data as objects within buckets, with features to organise data and manage access. For this lab a learner would store the 1HSG structure file as an object in a bucket, for the calculation or a later step to read. Not done here.[7]
  • Google Cloud documented, not executed herevendor-published Cloud Storage stores unstructured data as objects in buckets that belong to a project. The page lists storing datasets for analytics and machine learning among its uses. For this lab a learner would put the 1HSG structure file in a bucket and read it from there before the calculation. Not done here.[8]
  • Lab systems documented, not executed herepublisher-published eLabFTW is an open-source electronic lab notebook, used through the browser, for storing and organising research experiments. For this lab a learner would record the calculation as an experiment entry with the PDB ID, the structure file, the computed Matthews coefficient and solvent content, and the values in the RCSB entry. Not done here.[9]
