Counting what a curator-annotated text corpus holds

Parse the annotation files of BioRED, 600 PubMed abstracts annotated by curators (entities from pre-annotations they edited, relations by hand) with genes, diseases, chemicals, variants, species, cell lines and the relations between them, and rebuild the corpus statistics in Table 4 of the paper that describes it.

1. The question

How many gene, disease, chemical, variant, species and cell line mentions, how many unique concept identifiers and how many relations does the BioRED corpus hold in its training, development and test sets, and how are they stored?

Text-mining tools that find genes, drugs and diseases in papers are trained and tested on corpora like this one. Before you trust a score on a corpus you need to know what one mention, one identifier and one relation mean in its files. Rebuilding the published counts is the quickest way to learn that.

From: Table 4 (mentions and unique identifiers by entity type and split; relations and novel relations by split); Methods, section Data characteristics (per-document averages, relation-type shares)[1]

The paper presents BioRED as a benchmark for named entity recognition and relation extraction, steps that turn published text into linked facts for downstream text-mining applications. Entities are linked, where possible, to a database identifier in a public vocabulary (genes to NCBI Gene, variants to dbSNP, species to NCBI Taxonomy, diseases to MEDIC, a MeSH and OMIM merge, chemicals to MeSH, cell lines to Cellosaurus); unmatched cell lines carry '-'. None of the standards in this kit's registry applies.[1][2]

Not sourced yet: Where literature annotations and the knowledge bases built from them sit inside a company (for example literature screening for safety, or medical information) has no opened source yet. The system listed (warehouse) and the data type listed (protocol-document) are the closest ids in the kit's registry; a literature or annotated-text id is requested.

6. What goes wrong in practice

  • One span can carry several identifiers. A composite span such as "MMP-1, -2" is annotated whole, and its identifiers are written in one field separated by a comma with no space. Count the span once as a mention, but split the field before you count identifiers. [2]
  • A dash is not an identifier. Cell lines that cannot be matched in Cellosaurus get "-" as their identifier. Leave it out when you count unique identifiers, or it becomes one more concept. [2]
  • Unique counts do not add up across splits. Table 4 counts unique identifiers inside each column. The training, development and test columns add to more than the Total column, because the same gene or drug appears in more than one split. The All row, by contrast, is the sum of the six type rows. [1]
  • A relation joins concepts, not spans. A relation line names two identifiers and says they are related somewhere in that abstract. It does not point to the sentence or the spans. The same pair in two abstracts is two relations. [2][1]
  • Splitting composite fields changes the identifier count. The files hold 529 spans with more than one identifier. Split at the comma, the six classes hold 3869 unique identifiers, the number in Table 4. Left unsplit, the same files give 3962. Decide the rule before you count, and say which one you used. Seen in this lab's data; the notebook shows it.
  • A printed percentage may be cut, not rounded. Counted from the files, 17.7 percent of relations are Negative_Correlation; the paper's text prints 17. Its text also prints 69 percent novel, while its own Table 4 counts give 4532 of 6503, or 69.7 percent. Before you call a mismatch an error, compute the percentage from the paper's own counts. Seen in this lab's data; the notebook shows it.
  • The labels in the files are not the paper's names. The files use GeneOrGeneProduct, DiseaseOrPhenotypicFeature, ChemicalEntity, SequenceVariant, OrganismTaxon and CellLine. Map them to the paper's six classes once, and check that no label is left over. Seen in this lab's data; the notebook shows it.
  • One zip, three formats, two dates. BIORED.zip holds each split as PubTator, BioC XML and BioC JSON. Eight members are dated 2022-04-07; Test.BioC.XML is dated 2021-11-30. Count from one format and note which. This lab counts the PubTator files. Seen in this lab's data; the notebook shows it.

7. The same job on each platform

  • Open source executed here The lab's notebook opens the BioRED zip file, parses the PubTator-format annotation files and maps the type labels to the paper's six classes. It uses pandas to count mentions, unique identifiers and relations in each split and rebuilds Table 4 of the paper. This site's check runs it in a fresh Python process.[4]
  • Open source documented, not executed herepublisher-published DuckDB is an SQL database that runs inside the calling program, with no separate server, and reads CSV and Parquet files directly. For this lab a learner would save the parsed entity and relation rows as CSV or Parquet files and count them by type and split with GROUP BY queries. Not done here.[5]
  • Google Cloud documented, not executed herevendor-published BigQuery is a managed data platform for analysing data with SQL and Python. The page says data can be batch loaded from local files or Cloud Storage, in formats that include CSV and JSON. For this lab a learner would load the parsed entity and relation rows into tables and rebuild the Table 4 counts with aggregation queries. Not done here.[6]
