Medicines to follow

Pick one medicine and follow it through the guide: how it works, how it is given, the study that proved it, how it was approved and how its safety is watched.

imatinib (Glivec)

Novartis · Small molecule (tyrosine kinase inhibitor)

In one line

A tablet or capsule that blocks the enzymes driving some blood cancers and tumours.

How it works

Imatinib is a protein-tyrosine kinase inhibitor: it blocks tyrosine kinase enzymes that drive cancer cells to divide uncontrollably.[1] It treats several blood cancers and tumours, including chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML) and gastrointestinal stromal tumours (GIST).[1]

How it is given

Capsules or tablets taken by mouth with a meal and a large glass of water; the maximum dose is 800 mg a day.[1]

Key study

In 1,106 adults with CML, the cancer had got worse after five years in 16% of patients taking Glivec, against 28% taking interferon alpha plus cytarabine.[1]

Regulatory path

First authorised in the EU on 7 November 2001, held by Novartis Europharm.[1]

Safety

Side effects in more than 1 in 10 patients include weight increase, low blood cell counts, headache, nausea, fluid retention and fatigue.[1]

Sources

  1. Glivec (imatinib): EPAR overviewEuropean Medicines Agency

pembrolizumab (Keytruda)

Merck & Co. (MSD) · Monoclonal antibody (PD-1)

In one line

An antibody that releases a brake on the immune system so it can attack cancer.

How it works

Pembrolizumab is a monoclonal antibody that binds the PD-1 receptor on immune cells, so tumour cells cannot switch those cells off.[1] It treats several cancers, including melanoma, lung cancer and certain lymphomas, bladder, kidney and gastric cancers.[1]

How it is given

An infusion into a vein over 30 minutes every three or six weeks, or an injection under the skin every three or six weeks.[1]

Key study

In 540 previously treated patients with melanoma, the disease had not worsened two years after starting treatment in 16% on Keytruda, against less than 1% on chemotherapy.[1]

Regulatory path

First authorised in the EU on 17 July 2015, held by Merck Sharp & Dohme.[1]

Safety

The most common side effects of Keytruda alone, in more than 1 in 5 people, are tiredness, nausea and diarrhoea.[1] Most of its side effects come from the immune system itself, which can inflame organs and tissues; these can be serious, but most resolve with treatment or when Keytruda is stopped.[1]

Sources

  1. Keytruda (pembrolizumab): EPAR overviewEuropean Medicines Agency

semaglutide (Ozempic)

Novo Nordisk · GLP-1 analogue (GLP-1 receptor agonist)

In one line

A weekly injection that mimics a gut hormone to lower blood sugar in type 2 diabetes.

How it works

Semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist: it mimics the hormone GLP-1, which increases the insulin the pancreas releases after meals.[1] It treats adults with type 2 diabetes that is not well controlled, alongside diet and exercise.[1]

How it is given

An injection under the skin once a week from a prefilled pen, starting at 0.25 mg and rising to at most 1 mg.[1]

Key study

Five studies in over 4,000 patients lowered HbA1c by 1.2 to 1.8 percentage points over 10 to 13 months; in over 3,000 high-risk patients, heart attack, stroke or death occurred in 6.6% on Ozempic against 8.9% on placebo.[1]

Regulatory path

First authorised in the EU on 8 February 2018, held by Novo Nordisk.[1]

Safety

The most common side effects are digestive (diarrhoea, vomiting, nausea), described as mild or moderate and short-lived; serious worsening of diabetic retinopathy affects up to 1 in 10 people.[1]

Sources

  1. Ozempic (semaglutide): EPAR overviewEuropean Medicines Agency

trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu)

Daiichi Sankyo · Antibody-drug conjugate (HER2)

In one line

An antibody that carries a cell-killing drug straight to HER2-bearing cancer cells.

How it works

Trastuzumab binds HER2 on cancer cells; once inside the cell, the attached deruxtecan is released and blocks topoisomerase I, which cancer cells need to divide.[1] It treats adults with certain HER2-positive or HER2-low cancers, including some breast, lung and stomach cancers.[1]

How it is given

An infusion into a vein over 90 minutes once every three weeks; later infusions can take 30 minutes.[1]

Key study

In 184 patients with metastatic breast cancer who had already received two or more HER2-targeted treatments, the tumour shrank in about 61%.[1]

Regulatory path

Conditionally authorised in the EU on 18 January 2021, on less comprehensive data than normal because it meets an unmet need; the company must provide further data from two studies, and EMA reviews new information each year.[1]

Safety

Side effects in more than 1 in 10 people include nausea and vomiting, tiredness, hair loss, low blood cell counts and raised liver enzymes.[1] Severe interstitial lung disease (scarring in the lungs) and pneumonitis (lung inflammation) may occur in up to 1 in 10 people; the company gives healthcare professionals educational materials on the symptoms to watch for.[1]

Sources

  1. Enhertu (trastuzumab deruxtecan): EPAR overviewEuropean Medicines Agency

tisagenlecleucel (Kymriah)

Novartis · CAR-T cell therapy (ATMP)

In one line

A living medicine made from each patient’s own immune cells, reprogrammed to kill cancer.

How it works

The patient’s own T cells are modified in a laboratory to make a chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) that binds CD19 on cancer cells, so the cells can attack them.[1] It treats certain blood cancers: B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) in patients up to 25, and some lymphomas in adults.[1]

How it is given

White blood cells are collected from the patient, modified, and given back as a single infusion, only to that patient, after a short course of chemotherapy.[1]

Key study

In 92 children and young adults whose ALL had returned or not responded, around 66% had a complete response within 3 months; the 12-month survival likelihood was 70%.[1]

Regulatory path

First authorised in the EU on 23 August 2018, held by Novartis; it is an advanced therapy medicinal product (a gene therapy product).[1]

Safety

Serious side effects occur in most patients, most often cytokine release syndrome and low blood counts. Hospitals need trained staff, tocilizumab and emergency equipment; patients are closely monitored for 10 days and stay near a specialist hospital for at least 4 weeks. It is under additional monitoring.[1]

Sources

  1. Kymriah (tisagenlecleucel): EPAR overviewEuropean Medicines Agency

nusinersen (Spinraza)

Biogen · Antisense oligonucleotide

In one line

A short strand of genetic code, injected into the spine, that helps make a missing nerve protein.

How it works

Nusinersen is an antisense oligonucleotide that helps the SMN2 gene make full-length survival motor neuron (SMN) protein, the protein missing in spinal muscular atrophy.[1] It treats 5q spinal muscular atrophy, a rare genetic disease that weakens and wastes muscles.[1]

How it is given

An injection into the lower back, directly into the spinal fluid, by a doctor or nurse experienced in the procedure; treatment starts as soon as possible after diagnosis.[1]

Key study

In 121 babies with an average age of 7 months, 51% of those on Spinraza (37 of 73) progressed in head control, rolling, sitting, crawling, standing or walking after a year; none on a sham injection did.[1]

Regulatory path

First authorised in the EU on 30 May 2017, held by Biogen.[1]

Safety

The most common side effects, in more than 1 in 10 people, are headache, back pain and vomiting.[1]

Sources

  1. Spinraza (nusinersen): EPAR overviewEuropean Medicines Agency

COVID-19 mRNA vaccine (Comirnaty)

BioNTech and Pfizer · mRNA vaccine

In one line

An mRNA vaccine that teaches cells to make a virus protein, so the immune system learns to fight it.

How it works

The mRNA instructs some body cells to make the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein for a short time; the immune system then makes antibodies and T cells against it.[1] It prevents COVID-19 in people from 6 months of age.[1]

How it is given

An injection into the upper-arm muscle; the number of doses depends on age and vaccination history.[1]

Key study

In the original study of about 44,000 people aged 16 and over, there were 8 symptomatic cases among 18,198 vaccinated people against 162 among 18,325 given a dummy injection: 95% efficacy.[1]

Regulatory path

Conditionally authorised in the EU on 21 December 2020 and converted to a standard authorisation on 10 October 2022; BioNTech holds it. In the US, the FDA approved it for people 16 and over on 23 August 2021; it was developed by BioNTech and Pfizer.[1, 2]

Safety

Common side effects, which may affect more than 1 in 10 people, include pain and swelling at the injection site, tiredness, headache, muscle and joint pain, chills, fever and diarrhoea; most are mild to moderate and pass within a few days.[1] Myocarditis and pericarditis (inflammation of the heart muscle or of the membrane around the heart) may occur in up to 1 in 10,000 people.[1]

Sources

  1. Comirnaty (COVID-19 mRNA vaccine): EPAR overviewEuropean Medicines Agency
  2. Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 Vaccine COMIRNATY Receives Full U.S. FDA Approval for Individuals 16 Years and Older (press release, filed as Exhibit 99.1 to BioNTech Form 6-K)Pfizer and BioNTech, 2021

lenacapavir (Sunlenca)

Gilead · Small molecule (HIV capsid inhibitor)

In one line

An HIV medicine given as an injection just twice a year, after a short course of tablets.

How it works

Lenacapavir binds the proteins that form the HIV-1 capsid, the shell around the virus’s genetic material, and disrupts steps the virus needs to replicate.[1] It treats adults with multidrug-resistant HIV-1, combined with other HIV medicines, when no other suppressive regimen is possible.[1]

How it is given

Tablets by mouth on days 1, 2 and 8, then an injection under the skin every 26 weeks by a doctor or nurse.[1]

Key study

In patients who no longer responded to most HIV medicines, viral load fell meaningfully in 87.5% (21 of 24) on Sunlenca against 16.7% (2 of 12) on placebo; at 52 weeks, 83.3% (30 of 36) had viral load under 50 copies/mL.[1]

Regulatory path

First authorised in the EU on 17 August 2022, held by Gilead Sciences Ireland.[1]

Safety

The most common side effects are reactions at the injection site and nausea.[1]

Sources

  1. Sunlenca (lenacapavir): EPAR overviewEuropean Medicines Agency

finerenone (Kerendia)

Bayer · Small molecule (mineralocorticoid receptor blocker)

In one line

A once-daily tablet that protects the kidneys and heart by blocking a hormone receptor.

How it works

Finerenone binds the mineralocorticoid receptor and blocks processes that cause inflammation and scarring in the heart and kidneys.[1] It treats chronic kidney disease with albuminuria linked to type 2 diabetes, and symptomatic chronic heart failure with LVEF of 40% or more, in adults.[1]

How it is given

A tablet by mouth once a day; the dose depends on the condition and kidney function.[1]

Key study

In 5,662 patients with chronic kidney disease and type 2 diabetes, kidney function loss occurred in 18% on Kerendia (498 of 2,824) against 21% on placebo (600 of 2,838).[1]

Regulatory path

First authorised in the EU on 16 February 2022, held by Bayer AG.[1]

Safety

The most common side effect is high potassium in the blood (hyperkalaemia), which may affect more than 1 in 10 people.[1]

Sources

  1. Kerendia (finerenone): EPAR overviewEuropean Medicines Agency

onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma)

Novartis · Gene therapy (AAV9 vector)

In one line

A one-time gene therapy that gives nerve cells a working copy of the gene missing in spinal muscular atrophy.

How it works

Patients with spinal muscular atrophy have a defect in the SMN1 gene, which the body needs to make a protein that keeps the nerves controlling muscle movement working.[1] Zolgensma contains a functional copy of this gene; after the infusion it passes into the nerves and provides the gene needed to make enough of the protein.[1] It is for patients with 5q spinal muscular atrophy and mutations in both copies of SMN1, who have type 1 or up to 3 copies of the SMN2 gene.[2]

How it is made

The active substance is a non-replicating adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vector carrying the human SMN gene, produced in human embryonic kidney cells.[2] Before dosing, patients are tested for antibodies to AAV9 and for liver function.[2]

How it is given

Once, as an infusion into a vein lasting about 1 hour, in a clinic or hospital, supervised by a doctor experienced in spinal muscular atrophy.[1] Patients have liver and blood tests before and after, and receive corticosteroids to reduce the risk of side effects.[1]

Key study

In the main study, 20 of 22 babies given Zolgensma were alive and breathing without a permanent ventilator after 14 months; normally only about a quarter of untreated patients survive without one.[1] 14 of the 22 could sit unaided for at least 30 seconds after 18 months, a milestone untreated babies with severe disease never reach.[1]

Regulatory path

An orphan medicine. It received a conditional EU marketing authorisation on 18 May 2020, switched to a full authorisation on 17 May 2022 once the company supplied the additional evidence.[1] The marketing authorisation holder is Novartis Europharm Limited.[3]

Safety

The most common side effects are raised liver enzymes, liver injury, low blood platelets, raised troponin (a marker of heart muscle damage), fever and vomiting.[1] The company provides educational material to caregivers and runs a long-term study of safety and effectiveness.[1]

  2. Zolgensma: EPAR product information (summary of product characteristics)European Medicines Agency

exagamglogene autotemcel (Casgevy)

Vertex · Gene-edited cell therapy (CRISPR/Cas9; ATMP)

In one line

The patient’s own blood stem cells, edited with CRISPR/Cas9 so they make more fetal haemoglobin, given back once.

How it works

Casgevy is made from stem cells collected from the patient’s own blood.[1] The cells are edited with CRISPR/Cas9 so that they produce more fetal haemoglobin, which can make up for the missing or faulty adult haemoglobin.[1] It treats transfusion-dependent beta thalassaemia and severe sickle cell disease with recurrent painful crises, in patients 12 years and older, when a stem cell transplant is appropriate and an HLA-matched related donor (a relative with a matching tissue type) is not available.[2]

How it is given

Each batch is made for one patient and may only be given to that patient.[1] The patient first receives conditioning chemotherapy to clear the bone marrow, then a single infusion into a vein, with the dose based on body weight, in an approved centre experienced in stem cell transplantation.[1]

Key study

Interim results of two ongoing studies without a comparator, in patients aged 12 to 35. In beta thalassaemia, 39 of 42 patients kept haemoglobin above 9 g/dL without transfusions for at least 12 consecutive months.[1] In severe sickle cell disease, 28 of 29 patients had no painful crises for at least 12 consecutive months.[1]

Regulatory path

An orphan medicine and an advanced therapy (ATMP). It received a conditional EU marketing authorisation on 9 February 2024; the company must provide further evidence and the authorisation is reviewed every year.[1] The marketing authorisation holder is Vertex Pharmaceuticals (Ireland) Limited.[1]

Safety

The most common side effects, in more than 1 in 10 people, are headache, nausea and muscle and bone pain; most side effects came from the conditioning chemotherapy.[1] There is a theoretical risk of cancer from unintended changes to the genetic material, not seen so far, and a potential risk of bleeding from low platelets; both are followed in a 15-year registry-based study.[1]

Sources

  1. Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel): EPAR page and overviewEuropean Medicines Agency
  2. Casgevy: EPAR product information (summary of product characteristics)European Medicines Agency
