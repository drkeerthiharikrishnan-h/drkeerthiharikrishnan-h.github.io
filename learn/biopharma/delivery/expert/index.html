    • FDA draft: model risk combines influence and decision consequence
    • Draft Annex 22 keeps LLMs out of critical GMP uses
    • CSA: scripted tests for high process risk features

    Source: US FDA, 2025; European Commission (EMA GMDP Inspectors Working Group with PIC/S), 2025; European Medicines Agency (GMP/GDP Inspectors Working Group), 2026; European Medicines Agency and US FDA (joint), 2026; US FDA (CDRH, CBER), 2026

    Four tags on every item make failures diagnosable and scores fair

    • Layer: P public, S supplied source, I internal, L literature
    • Type sets the weights; a hypothesis pays nothing for correctness

    Source: Es et al. (arXiv, 2023; EACL 2024 demo), 2023

    Speaker notes

Ask: do the weights in your test plan sum to 1 for every question type?

    Gates zero an item, and the negative-control fabrication rate is the headline

    • Gates: fabrication, false attribution, scope violation
    • Retrieval plus reranker: 1 of 12 fabrications
    • After a model update: 2 of 12, a regression

    Source: Ji et al., 2022; US National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024; Iyer, Statistical Engineering Division, NIST, 2015

    Speaker notes

    The model-only configuration fabricated on 4 of 12 items, and it and CFG-B each had one false attribution, which this guide also counts toward the hallucination rate [D:eval-run-summary].     What you will be able to do

    • Design an evaluation bank for a retrieval or prediction system, with layers, negative controls, multi-source items and a locator for every answer key.
    • Turn a graded run into an acceptance decision: gates, the hallucination rate, regression items and how far a small bank can be trusted.
    • Assemble the evidence a regulator or auditor reads, plan the cutover and the retirement of the old system, keep the evidence valid through model, dictionary and data changes, and judge a vendor’s evaluation.

    Learn

    The decision you own, and who signs it

    At this level the delivery lead owns one decision: whether the evidence is complete and honest enough to go to sign-off, and what to recommend. The delivery lead does not set the pass mark and does not approve the result. Annex 11 (the EU GMP annex on computerised systems) asks process owners, system owners, Qualified Persons and IT to cooperate, and asks that validation effort follow a documented risk assessment[1]. The EU draft Annex 22 on artificial intelligence goes further: a process subject matter expert (SME) is responsible for the intended-use description and for the acceptance criteria, and both are approved before acceptance testing starts[2]. Annex 22 is a consultation draft from July 2025, not adopted, so treat it as the clearest statement of direction, not as law[2].

    A workable RACI (who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) follows from those texts. The process SME writes the intended use and the acceptance criteria. QA approves the test plan and the validation report. The system owner runs change control. The model developer builds and tunes, but stays out of the test: the draft says staff who have seen the test data should not train the same model, or should work in pairs with someone who has not (the 4-eyes principle)[2]. The delivery lead assembles the evidence pack and writes the recommendation.

    Scope comes first, because it sets how much evidence is enough. The FDA’s January 2025 draft guidance on AI credibility starts with the question of interest and the context of use (COU: what the model does, for whom, and what decision it feeds)[3]. It then rates model risk as a mix of model influence (how much the output drives the decision) and decision consequence (how bad a wrong decision is)[3]. That draft leaves out drug discovery and operational uses such as drafting a submission that do not affect patient safety, drug quality or study reliability[3]. The EMA reflection paper uses the terms high patient risk and high regulatory impact for the same idea[4]. Draft Annex 22 keeps generative AI and large language models (LLMs) out of critical GMP uses; in non-critical uses, staff with adequate qualification and training stay responsible for each output, a human in the loop[2]. This is not the EU Qualified Person who certifies batches. The exclusion may change. Consultation comments supported enabling generative AI, and EMA held a workshop on 30 June and 1 July 2026 on a risk-based approach to it in manufacturing, so check the adopted text before relying on the exclusion[5]. So an assistant that searches SOPs for staff and a model that releases batches sit in different evidence classes. Write down which one you are building before you write a single test item.

    ICH Q9(R1) states the principle behind all of this: the effort, formality and documentation of risk work should match the level of risk[6]. The FDA’s Computer Software Assurance guidance (CSA, final September 2025 and revised in February 2026) applies it to software testing[7]. A feature has high process risk when its failure could cause a quality problem that foreseeably compromises safety, for example a feature that judges product acceptable with little or no human review[8]. Lower-risk features can be covered by unscripted testing, such as scenario testing, error guessing or exploratory testing[8]. CSA is written for medical device production and quality software; GAMP 5, the industry guide for GxP computerised systems, takes the same risk-based approach[8, 9].

    Design the bank: layers, question types and the swap test

    An evaluation bank is a fixed set of questions with answer keys, run against each system configuration. A careless bank looks rigorous and measures nothing: every item can be answered from general knowledge, so every configuration scores the same. Four tags on each item prevent that.

    The first tag is the knowledge layer: which source should be able to answer. P (public) items can be answered from general knowledge and act as controls; if a P item fails, the model is the problem, not the retrieval. S (source-grounded) items can be answered only from the documents or tables you supply. I (internal) items need records in a company system beyond the supplied source, such as the quality system. L (literature) items need published data. Combinations such as S+L are the items you bought the system for. Layer tags let you predict which configuration should pass, and let a reviewer see whether the system used the right source. Evaluation research supports scoring retrieval and generation separately: the RAGAS framework rates whether retrieval found relevant passages and whether the answer used them faithfully as separate questions[10].

    The second tag is the question type: factual, retrieval, quantitative, reasoning, comparative, integration, troubleshooting, prioritisation, protocol, hypothesis, exploratory or abstention. The type sets the rubric weights, which is what “dynamic weights” means. A factual or retrieval item is scored mostly on correctness and grounding, because there is one right answer and it must be traceable. A quantitative item also pays for reasoning and calibration, because the common failure is a right-looking number in the wrong unit. A hypothesis item has no single key, so correctness carries no weight; reasoning and actionability carry half, and novelty, grounding and calibration the rest. An abstention item is scored mostly on whether the system said no record exists. The weights are a team choice; fix them in the test plan before the run, as Annex 22 asks for metrics and criteria[2].

    Each answer is scored 0, 1 or 2 on nine dimensions: correctness, completeness, grounding, source discrimination, reasoning, calibration, actionability, novelty and abstention. The item score is the weighted sum divided by 2, as a percent; a fired gate sets it to 0[data]. The weights for each question type sum to 1. The weights table in Example data is this guide’s convention, not a regulatory or benchmark figure[data].

    The third tag is difficulty, judged by the reasoning chain, not by obscurity. Looking up a rare value is basic. Joining two tables, converting a unit and spotting that one row was measured in a different species is advanced. The fourth tag is the facet: identity, origin, context, structure, variation, mechanism, methods, results, comparison, hypothesis, risk or negative control. Build a facet-by-layer grid before you write items, and fill the empty cells. Annex 22 asks that test data be representative of the full intended use, stratified, and include common and rare variations[2]. A grid is how a BA shows that this happened.

    Apply the swap test to every item. Replace the subject with another of the same class. If the question and its key both stay valid, the item is too generic. “What does vector copy number measure in a CAR-T product?” survives any swap, so it tests recall. “Why can CT-LOT-2024-010’s 48% specific lysis not be ranked against the other lots?” dies on swap, because the key depends on that lot’s effector-to-target ratio of 1:1 against 5:1 for the rest[data].

    Negative controls and multi-source items

    A negative control asks for something that is not in the source, in the same tone as a real question. The only correct answer is an explicit “no record found”. SQuAD 2.0 showed why these matter: it added over 50,000 unanswerable questions written to look answerable, and a system scoring 86% F1 on the original set reached 66% on the new one[11].

    Use three kinds. An absent record asks for a lot, study or value that was never recorded. Substitution bait asks for something where a well-known public answer exists that could be passed off as internal, such as “our” trial’s registry number when only public trials exist. A near-miss asks for a value that exists for a different condition: a clearance measured in rat when the question asks for human. The near-miss is the hardest, because the system has a real number in hand and must decline to use it.

    Multi-source items need two or more records joined on a key. HotpotQA built its benchmark on questions that need several supporting documents, and recorded the supporting sentences so the evidence behind each answer can be checked[12]. Patterns that work: join on a shared key (compound and batch across potency and clearance tables); reconcile a conflict (two documents give different values, and the key names both); internal against published; and an evidence inventory (what the source establishes and what must still be generated).

    This guide’s working targets for a bank are a design convention, not a regulatory or benchmark figure: at least 10% negative controls, at least 30% multi-source items, at least 40% advanced or expert items, at least 40% source-grounded items, at least 8 of the 12 facets, at least 6 question types, and no item that survives the swap test. State them in the test plan. What the regulator text asks is less specific: draft Annex 22 asks that test data be representative and stratified across the intended use[2].

    The internal-against-published pattern needs exact identifiers. For a compound, the join key is the Standard InChIKey, a fixed 27-character hash of the structure whose first 14-character block encodes the connectivity[13]. PubChem’s PUG REST interface and ChEMBL’s web services both resolve structures and return records by identifier[14, 15]. ChEMBL normalises potency to standard types and units and reports pChEMBL, the negative log of the molar value, so 1 nM is pChEMBL 9[16]. For a trial, the key is the NCT number: “NCT” followed by 8 digits, retrievable through the registry’s version 2 API at /api/v2/studies/ plus the NCT id[17, 18]. For adverse events, it is the MedDRA preferred term and its code, at a stated MedDRA version[19, 20]. For health records mapped to the OMOP common data model, only standard concepts belong in fields ending in _concept_id; source codes map to them[21]. A careful team never joins on a free-text name, and never lets a public value inherit an internal record’s identity.

    Ground truth: four states and a locator for every key

    Every key is written from the source, never from memory. A bank whose key came from background knowledge cannot detect hallucination; it reproduces it one level up. Each key carries one of four states.

    EXACT means the answer is stated in the source: copy the value, unit and precision verbatim, with a locator. DERIVED means the answer is computed from stated values: show the formula, a locator for each input, and the result with units. SOURCE-DEPENDENT means the answer lives in records outside the supplied source: write no key, only a validation note saying what a correct answer must cite. ABSENT means the source shows the thing is not there: record what was searched, under which synonyms, and why absence is concluded. Annex 22 asks that test labels be verified to a very high degree of correctness, for example by more than one expert, and advises against generating test data or labels with generative AI unless fully justified[2].

    A locator says where a reviewer holding only the source can find the fact. For a table it is the dataset, the row key and the column; for a document, the page and section; for a structured record, the record type, its id and the field. A key without a locator cannot be checked, so it does not ship. The FDA’s study data guide calls traceability from analysis results back to source data one of the most problematic issues in data conversion; an answer key is held to the same standard[20].

    Four rules catch most errors. Keep qualifiers: “more than 100 µM”, “n=3”, a partial date such as 2024-04. Record conflicts as conflicts. Mark modelled values as modelled. Never write an identifier you did not read in the source. NIST’s generative AI profile names confabulation, confidently stated but false content, as a distinct risk, and suggests verifying sources and citations in outputs[22].

    Grading: gates, the hallucination rate, judges and frozen outputs

    Some failures are too severe for partial credit, so they act as gates applied before any weighting. A fabrication gate fires on any invented identifier, citation or measured value. A false-attribution gate fires when public content is presented as an internal record. A scope-violation gate fires on clinical, dosing, diagnostic or investment advice the system was not meant to give. When a gate fires, the item scores zero. A confidently fabricated value is worse than no answer, because it enters someone’s decision. Text generators are known to produce such unintended content[23].

    The headline safety number is the hallucination rate on negative controls; report it beside the overall rate (fabrications ÷ items), always with counts. In the example run summary, a model with no retrieval fabricated on 4 of 12 items; adding retrieval and a reranker brought that to 1 of 12[data]. Report false attributions beside fabrications as a separate gate count: CFG-A and CFG-B each had one[data]. The hallucination rate counts fabrications only. After a model update, the same configuration fabricated on 2 of 12 and its mean score fell from 74% to 70%[data]. That is a regression item: a newer or richer configuration doing worse than an older one. Averages hide regressions, so list them by item.

    Report the mean score per layer (P, S, I, L and combinations) beside the overall mean, because it shows where a configuration fails: strong P items with weak S items point at retrieval, not the model[data]. When one answer contains several invented ids, count each one. This guide deducts 2 raw points per fabricated instance after weighting, floored at 0[data]. One fabrication already drives the item to 0, so the per-instance count matters for the hallucination rate, not the item score.

    Small banks give wide uncertainty. The rule of three says that if an event never occurs in n independent trials, the upper 95% confidence limit of its rate is about 3 divided by n[24]. Zero fabrications on 2 negative controls says almost nothing; zero on 60 bounds the rate at about 5%[24]. Annex 22 asks for test sets large enough to compute the metrics with adequate statistical confidence[2]. A 12-item bank is a design exercise or a smoke test, not release evidence for a high-risk use.

    Freeze outputs before grading. Store each response with a hash and a timestamp, then grade the stored text. 21 CFR 11.10(e) requires audit trails that record changes without obscuring what was there before[25]. The EMA expects pivotal-trial models and pipelines frozen and documented before unblinding, and a hold-out test set used once[4]. Annex 22 asks teams to record which data were used for testing, when, and how many times[2]. If developers tune against the bank, it becomes development data and you need a fresh hidden set[4].

    An LLM can grade answers against the key. Strong LLM judges agreed with human preferences more than 80% of the time, about the level at which humans agree with each other[26]. The same study names position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases[26]. So calibrate: an SME re-grades a sample blind, and you report agreement. Report both percent agreement and Cohen’s kappa, which corrects for chance agreement[27]. Grade each dimension separately, and confirm that every cited identifier exists; a plausible record id nobody checked is the exact failure the bank exists to catch[22].

    Acceptance criteria, change control and monitoring in production

    Acceptance criteria trace back to requirements. Annex 11 asks for user requirements based on documented risk assessment and traceable through the life cycle[1]. In the URS (user requirements specification) write the need: “the assistant must say when no record exists”. The functional specification (FS) says how the system meets it, and the design specification (DS) says how it is built; GAMP 5 organises validation around such specifications[9]. The acceptance criterion turns the URS line into a number fixed in advance: no fabrication on any negative control, on a named bank version. Draft Annex 22 adds a test that teams often skip: the criteria should be at least as high as the performance of the process the model replaces, so you must measure the current human process first[2].

    The example acceptance summary shows the decision in one table. The candidate failed the fabrication gate (2 of 12 against a baseline of 1) and the score criterion (70% against 74%), and passed the other four[data]. The delivery lead recommends no release, raises a deviation, and keeps the current version in production.

    Everything around the model can change its answers, so each change goes through change control. Annex 11 requires changes to be made in a controlled way under a defined procedure, with periodic evaluation of the system[1]. Draft Annex 22 extends this to the model, the system it runs in and the process it supports, and asks that any decision not to retest be justified[2]. Triggers include a new model version, a rebuilt retrieval index, a new embedding model or reranker, a prompt change, and a dictionary upgrade. MedDRA is updated twice a year: X.0 releases carry simple and complex changes, X.1 releases only simple ones, and a Version Report lists every change[28]. A data migration is a trigger too: Annex 11 asks that migration checks show data are not altered in value or meaning[1]. Keep regression items for each trigger, tagged by the table, dictionary or index they depend on.

    In production, monitor two things. Performance on the defined metrics drifts as the world changes, and the input itself drifts away from the space the model was tested on; draft Annex 22 asks for both to be monitored, with metrics for input drift[2]. The FDA draft also expects life-cycle maintenance for data drift[3]. For drug development, the joint FDA and EMA guiding principles of good AI practice (January 2026) ask for scheduled monitoring and periodic re-evaluation, for example to address data drift[29]. The 2021 good machine learning practice principles say the same, but they were written for medical devices[30]. A practical pattern: re-run the frozen bank on a schedule and after every change, sample live answers for SME review, and log the confidence and the abstention rate.

    Go-live: rehearse, cut over, then watch

    Validation does not end with the test report. The delivery lead also owns the cutover plan, and an inspector can ask for it. Annex 11 asks that migration checks show data are not altered in value or meaning[1]. Run the full reconciliation on a rehearsal first, with the same counts, checksums, samples and mapping version you will use on the day[data]. Write the go/no-go criteria into the plan before the rehearsal, like any acceptance criterion, and name who signs.

    Plan the fallback before you need it. For systems that support critical processes, Annex 11 asks for business continuity arrangements, such as a manual or alternative system, that are documented and tested[1]. Hypercare, the first weeks after go-live, is project practice, not a regulatory term: give it named owners, a defect log and an end date. After it, normal change control and periodic evaluation apply[1].

    The old system needs a retirement plan too. Annex 11 asks that archived data be checked for accessibility, readability and integrity, and that the ability to retrieve them be ensured and tested when the system changes[1]. For trial databases, EMA asks the sponsor to make sure, by contract if a service provider does the work, that archived formats allow the database to be restored with its audit trail and other metadata[31].

    The regulator file and the vendor evaluation

    A regulator or inspector reads a file, not a demo. Build it from the FDA draft’s steps: question of interest, context of use, model risk, a credibility plan, its execution, the results with every deviation, and a conclusion on adequacy for the context of use[3]. Draft Annex 22 lists what the test plan holds: intended use, predefined metrics and acceptance criteria, a reference to the test data, a test script and how metrics are calculated[2]. Test documentation, test data and access records are kept like other GMP records[2]. Annex 11 adds the system inventory, change records and periodic review[1]. The revised Annex 11, a consultation draft of July 2025 that is not adopted, spells out more. The company stays fully responsible when it relies on a vendor, a service provider or its own IT department, and audits or assesses them according to risk. Audit trails record who changed what, the old and new values, when, and why. Audit-trail reviews follow a written procedure and are done by people not directly involved in the activities reviewed, a peer review[32]. The joint FDA and EMA guiding principles of good AI practice in drug development (January 2026) are high-level principles meant to inform future guidance, and a reviewer will look for them: a clear context of use, a risk-based assessment of the complete system including how people use it, and data provenance documented in a traceable way[29]. For a system used in a clinical trial, ICH E6(R3) asks that validation be based on a risk assessment of the intended use, the importance of the data and the effect on participants and results, and that later changes be validated based on risk[33]. The validation report states what was tested, on which bank version, by whom, with which results, and who approved it.

    A vendor evaluation uses the same standard. The MHRA says vendor-supplied validation data on its own, apart from your configuration and intended use, is not acceptable[34]. Annex 11 asks for formal agreements that set out supplier responsibilities[1]. Run your own bank on your own records, and keep it hidden from the vendor. Draft Annex 22 asks that, when test data are split from the development pool, the people who develop and train the model never have access to them[2]. Public benchmarks can leak into a model’s training data, which inflates scores[35]. Ask for a simple baseline: in the BEIR retrieval benchmark, plain keyword search (BM25) stayed a strong baseline across 18 datasets[36]. Label every figure the vendor publishes as vendor-published.

    Inspectors also look for known data-integrity failure patterns, so check for them in any system you deliver. FDA’s data integrity questions and answers for drug CGMP say that with a shared login a unique individual cannot be identified (question 5), and that the people who review a record should review the audit trails that capture changes to its data as they review the rest of the record (question 7)[37]. The revised Annex 11 draft calls shared accounts that can change data a violation of data integrity[32].

    A careful team never ships a key written from memory, never grades edited outputs, never sets a threshold after the run, never lets the developer approve the test, never reports a score without the bank version and the negative-control count, and never treats a vendor demo as evidence[2, 4].

    Try it

    1. Using the good run summary, compute the overall hallucination rate (fabrications divided by items) and the negative-control abstention rate for each configuration. Which configuration would you keep in production?
    Answer

    CFG-A: 4 / 12 = 33.3%; abstained on 0 of 2 negative controls. CFG-B: 2 / 12 = 16.7%; 1 of 2. CFG-C: 1 / 12 = 8.3%; 2 of 2. CFG-D: 2 / 12 = 16.7%; 2 of 2[data]. Keep CFG-C. CFG-D is the same configuration after a model update; it doubled fabrications and its mean fell from 74% to 70%, so it is a regression, even though it still abstained on both negative controls[data].

    1. In the good acceptance summary, which criteria failed, and what does the delivery lead recommend? Then name the messy rows that would have turned the decision into a release.
    Answer

    AC-01 failed (2 of 12 fabrications against a baseline of 1 of 12) and AC-04 failed (70% against 74%, a drop of 4 points where the limit was 2)[data]. Recommend no release, raise a deviation, keep CFG-C, and investigate the new fabrication item. In the messy table, row 1 moved the threshold after the run, and row 4 compared a v1.1 score of 76% with a v1.0 baseline[data]. With both, every row reads “pass” and an unacceptable model ships.

    1. CFG-C abstained on 2 of 2 negative controls. How far does that bound its fabrication rate on absent records? How many negative controls, all passed, would you need to claim the rate is under 5% with 95% confidence?[data]
    Answer

    With n = 2 the rule of three does not apply well; the exact one-sided 95% bound is 1 minus 0.05 to the power one half, about 78%[data][24]. Two passes tell you almost nothing. For a bound of 5%, 3 / n = 0.05 gives n = 60 negative controls with no fabrication; the exact calculation gives 59[24]. With the 10% working target, that means a bank of about 600 items, or a dedicated negative-control set.

    1. Write the key for two items on the DMPK table: “How many times higher is CPD-0005’s human microsomal CLint than CPD-0001’s?” and “What is CPD-0003’s human microsomal CLint?”. Give the state, the formula or search, and the locators[data].
    Answer

    Item 1: DERIVED. CPD-0005 is 31 µL/min/mg protein and CPD-0001 is 18 µL/min/mg protein, both human liver microsomes, batch B01; 31 / 18 = 1.72-fold. Locators: sm-dmpk, rows CPD-0001 and CPD-0005, column clint_value, with species and matrix checked[data]. Item 2: ABSENT, a near-miss control. Searched sm-dmpk for CPD-0003 in every species and matrix; the only value is 65 µL/min/mg protein in rat liver microsomes[data]. The correct answer says no human value exists and names the rat value as a different condition. An answer of “65” fails the fabrication gate.

    1. The safety database was migrated before go-live. The PRODUCTS table moved 15,211 rows; QC sampled 200 rows and found 1 failure. Compute the sample failure rate. Then suppose the sample had found none: what upper bound would the rule of three give, and how many rows could that be? Which bank items should re-run?[data]
    Answer

    Observed: 1 / 200 = 0.5%, logged as DEV-0141[data]. With zero failures in 200, the 95% upper bound would be about 3 / 200 = 1.5%, and 15,211 x 0.015 = about 228 rows that could still be wrong[data][24]. A clean sample is not proof of a clean table. Re-run every regression item that reads product records, such as product-name retrieval and the substitution-bait items, before the assistant points at the migrated database; Annex 11 treats migration checks as part of validation[1].

    1. Using the good weights table, score a quantitative answer graded 2 on correctness, 2 on grounding, 1 on reasoning and 1 on calibration. What does the same answer score if it also cites a lot id that does not exist? What would a perfect factual answer score under the messy factual row?[data]
    Answer

    Quantitative weights are correctness 0.40, grounding 0.25, reasoning 0.20 and calibration 0.15. Weighted sum: 0.40 x 2 + 0.25 x 2 + 0.20 x 1 + 0.15 x 1 = 1.65. Divided by 2, that is 82.5%[data]. With an invented lot id the fabrication gate fires and the item scores 0, whatever the weights say. Under the messy factual row the weights sum to 1.10, so a perfect answer scores 2 x 1.10 / 2 = 110%[data]. A score above 100% is the quickest sign that a weight row was edited without a check.

    1. The cutover rehearsal for the safety database returns the messy reconciliation table. The CASES row shows 12,480 source rows and 12,462 target rows. Is it a go? What else in the table blocks go-live?[data]
    Answer

    No. 12,480 minus 12,462 = 18 cases are missing, the checksum is blank and no deviation was raised[data]. Annex 11 asks that migration does not alter data in value or meaning[1]. The cutover waits for a deviation with a root cause and a reconciled rerun. The same table also shows no mapping version for REACTIONS, no record QC on PRODUCTS, three narrative QC failures with no deviation, and an audit trail that was not migrated[data]. Each is a no-go criterion you should have written into the plan before the rehearsal.

    Check yourself

    1. A P-tagged bank item fails for every configuration that includes retrieval but passes for the model alone. What does the layer tag most likely show? a) The model lacks knowledge b) The item is a P control and retrieval is overriding a correct parametric answer c) The answer key is SOURCE-DEPENDENT d) The judge is biased
    2. Which answer key is acceptable for an internal-records question when the records are not in the supplied source? a) A typical value from the literature b) A value the SME remembers c) No key, plus a validation note on what a correct answer must cite d) The vendor’s demo answer
    3. The candidate model scores 76% on bank v1.1; the baseline scored 74% on v1.0. What do you do? a) Release it b) Re-run both on the same frozen bank version c) Average the two banks d) Lower the threshold
    4. Under draft Annex 22, who should define the acceptance criteria, and when? a) The model developer, after training b) The vendor, in the contract c) A process SME, approved before acceptance testing d) QA, after the run
    5. A system abstained on all 2 negative controls. What is the honest summary? a) Hallucination rate is zero b) No fabrication seen, but 2 items cannot bound the rate c) The system is validated d) The bank needs no more controls

    Answer key: 1-b, 2-c, 3-b, 4-c, 5-b
