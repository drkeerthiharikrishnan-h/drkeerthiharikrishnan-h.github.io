    What you will be able to do

    • Write a URS for a retrieval or prediction system that names the question, the label, the test split and measurable acceptance criteria.
    • Read an evaluation table (recall@k, MRR, nDCG, AUROC, PR-AUC, pLDDT) and say whether a number supports the decision it is quoted for.
    • Plan the change control, audit trail and sign-off for models, indexes and dictionaries in a GxP setting.

    Learn

    Start from the decision, the label and the positives you have

    The FDA draft guidance on AI credibility (January 2025, not final) starts each assessment with the question of interest and the context of use, meaning the model’s specific role and scope[1]. It then rates model risk from two things: how much the output influences the decision, and how serious a wrong decision would be[1]. For delivery, this is page one of the user requirements specification (URS). EU GMP Annex 15 defines the URS as the owner, user and engineering requirements needed for a feasible design that meets the intended purpose, and says it should be a point of reference throughout the validation life cycle[2].

    Next, write down the label: the answer the model learns to reproduce. For a property model it might be “pIC50 of 6.0 or more in assay ASSAY-017 version 2”. pIC50 is the negative log10 of the molar IC50, so 1 nM is 9 and 1 µM is 6[3]. The OECD principles for QSAR models (quantitative structure-activity relationship models, which predict a property from chemical structure) ask first for a defined endpoint and an unambiguous algorithm[4]. A label that just says “active” pools different assays and cut-offs, and the model learns the mixture.

    Then count the positives, which are scarce because the development funnel is steep. Across 9,704 programmes tracked from 2011 to 2020, the likelihood of approval from Phase I was 7.9%[5]. A model that predicts “approved” from Phase I data sees about 8 positives per 100 records. In the lab, the MoleculeNet benchmark reports that learned models still struggle with data scarcity and highly imbalanced classes[6].

    So the business analyst (BA) puts a data readiness section in the URS before any model section: source systems, record counts, number of positives, the label rule with its version, known gaps. A later change to the label rule is a requirement change under change control[7].

    Embeddings: text, molecules and proteins as vectors

    An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that a model produces for an item, so that similar items get nearby vectors. Sentence-BERT produces text embeddings compared by cosine similarity, and it cut the time to find the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences from about 65 hours to about 5 seconds[8]. No text embedding model wins everywhere: the MTEB benchmark tested 33 models on 58 datasets and found none dominant across tasks[9]. So test embedding models on your own documents, not on a leaderboard.

    Molecules have their own embeddings. ECFPs (extended-connectivity fingerprints) are circular fingerprints built for structure-activity modelling, and each feature records the presence of a particular substructure[10]. The algorithm can be tuned to produce different fingerprint types[10], so the type and settings belong in the configuration record: another fingerprint gives another similarity for the same pair.

    Proteins have language-model embeddings. ESM-2 is described by its developers as a general-purpose protein language model that can predict structure, function and other properties from single sequences[11]. Its largest checkpoint has 15 billion parameters, 48 layers and an embedding size of 5,120 numbers per residue[11]. The published example code takes one vector per residue (amino acid) and averages them into one vector per sequence[11]. The URS says which of the two a protein model uses.

    Long documents are split into chunks before embedding. The original retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) paper split each Wikipedia article into disjoint 100-word chunks, 21 million in total[12]. A survey of RAG systems reports common chunk sizes of 100, 256 or 512 tokens, and notes that larger chunks carry more context but also more noise and cost[13]. In a protocol, a clinical study report (CSR) or a standard operating procedure (SOP), a fixed-size cut can separate an inclusion criterion from its exception. Chunk on sections, tables and numbered criteria where possible, and test the choice on a gold set.

    Each chunk also carries metadata: document id, version, effective status, section and any controlled codes. The same survey describes adding metadata to the index so that retrieval can filter on it[13]. In regulated content the filters do real work. “Effective SOP versions only” removes superseded text. A trial filter uses the NCT number, which is “NCT” followed by 8 digits[14]. In OMOP data, only standard concepts (marked “S”) go in the concept-id fields[15]. Filter on the code, not on the free text.

    Changing the embedding model means every vector is recomputed. Treat it as a data migration. Annex 11 expects checks that migrated data are not altered in value or meaning[7]. For an index that means the chunk count reconciles from source to target, a sample is checked by hand, and the gold-set evaluation is re-run before release.

    Retrieval: lexical, dense and hybrid

    Lexical retrieval matches query words to document words. BM25 is one of the most successful text-retrieval algorithms; it came out of the probabilistic relevance framework, grounded in work done in the 1970s and 1980s[16]. It is exact about strings: a query for LOT-2024-007 finds documents containing that string. In the BEIR benchmark of 18 datasets, BM25 was a robust baseline and dense models often underperformed[17].

    Dense retrieval compares embeddings. It finds paraphrases, such as “liver injury” for “hepatotoxicity”, but it can miss an exact code. The RAG paper stored document embeddings in a FAISS index searched with a hierarchical navigable small world approximation, a method built for speed[12]. Vector databases do the same job with metadata filters added. “Approximate” means the index can miss a vector an exhaustive search would return, so index type and settings are part of the validated configuration.

    Hybrid retrieval runs both and merges the lists. Lexical and dense retrieval capture different signals and can be combined[13]. A common merge is reciprocal rank fusion (RRF): each document scores the sum of 1/(k + rank) over the lists, with k at its default of 60 in tools such as Elasticsearch[18, 19]. Worked example: document A is rank 1 in BM25 and rank 4 in dense, so it scores 1/61 + 1/64 = 0.0164 + 0.0156 = 0.0320. Document B is rank 3 and rank 1, so it scores 1/63 + 1/61 = 0.0159 + 0.0164 = 0.0323[19]. RRF uses only ranks, so the two score scales never need to be made comparable.

    Every returned passage carries a locator: document id, version, section or page. A reviewer must be able to open the source. The EMA expects the trial master file to support document identification, version history, search and retrieval[20]. A careful team never lets the system answer from a superseded version. In the retrieval example table, query Q-004 returned PROT-001 v2 at rank 1 while the gold record was v3[data]. A version filter would have removed that hit.

    Reranking: a slower second reader for the top of the list

    First-stage retrieval is built for speed; a reranker reads fewer candidates more carefully. A cross-encoder takes the query and one passage together and outputs one relevance score. Nogueira and Cho used BERT this way and reached the top of the MS MARCO passage leaderboard, a 27% relative gain in MRR@10 over the previous best[21].

    The cost is the reason it is not used everywhere. Because every query-passage pair passes through the full network, BERT rerankers raise compute cost by orders of magnitude[22]. In BEIR, reranking gave the best zero-shot results on average but at high computational cost[17]. The design that follows: retrieve a wide candidate list cheaply, then rerank only that list to a short top-k.

    Reranking fixes top-k but not recall. If the gold passage sits at rank 12 of 100 candidates, recall@5 counts a miss, and a reranker can lift it into the top 5. If the first stage never retrieved it, nothing downstream can recover it. Acceptance criteria therefore sit at two levels: first-stage recall at the candidate depth, and final recall@k after reranking[17].

    Grounded generation and abstention

    RAG joins a generator model to retrieved documents. Its authors wrote that models relying only on their trained parameters struggle to give provenance and to update knowledge[12]. Grounding means the answer is built from the retrieved passages, and each claim cites a passage locator.

    Abstention is the second half: when no record answers, the system says so. Ragas scores separately whether retrieval found relevant passages and whether the generator used them faithfully[23]. The RAG survey lists negative rejection, declining when the retrieved content lacks the answer, as an ability to test[13]. So the gold set includes negative controls: questions with no answering record. In the example table, Q-005 is such a control; the good run abstained, while the messy run returned CASE-2024-0199 and marked it “pass”[data].

    Large gold sets are often graded by a model acting as judge. In one study, strong LLM judges agreed with human preferences more than 80% of the time, about the level at which humans agree with each other[24]. That is a general benchmark. The judge’s agreement with your subject-matter experts is measured on your gold set before its grades count, and every grade is attributable to a named person or a versioned judge[25].

    These ideas become URS lines a tester can execute. For example: the system shall show document id, version and section for every cited passage; the system shall abstain on every negative-control query in the gold set; recall@5 on the answerable queries shall meet the threshold the process owner sets from the risk assessment. Each line traces to a test and its evidence[7].

    Predictions are not measurements

    ADMET models (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity) predict a property from structure. The OECD’s third principle asks for a defined domain of applicability, because QSAR models are reductionist and only predict reliably for the kinds of structures, properties and mechanisms they were built on[4]. In practice the domain is a rule, such as a minimum similarity to the nearest training compound; outside it a prediction is an extrapolation, and the screen should say so.

    How the test set was chosen decides what a score means. MoleculeNet notes that random splitting, common in machine learning, is often not correct for chemical data[6]. Scaffold splitting puts structurally different molecules in different sets and is harder; time splitting trains on older data and tests on newer data, which mimics real use[6]. Sheridan found time-split results closer to prospective performance, while random selection was too optimistic[26]. Leakage is any route by which test information reaches training. The EMA reflection paper says a hold-out test set is used once, a new independent test set is needed after further development, and an early split, before normalisation, is strongly encouraged[27]. The same paper prefers metrics that are insensitive to class imbalance, such as the Matthews correlation coefficient[27]. The FDA draft also expects test data independent of development data[1].

    Joining internal compounds to public data follows a fixed pattern. The internal compound (CPD-0001) gets a Standard InChIKey computed from its registered structure. An InChIKey has 27 characters; the first 14-character block hashes the connectivity (the skeleton) and the second block hashes stereochemistry and isotopes[28]. PubChem’s PUG REST service accepts the inchikey namespace and returns the compound id; for aspirin it returns CID 2244 and InChIKey BSYNRYMUTXBXSQ-UHFFFAOYSA-N[29]. ChEMBL addresses molecules by ChEMBL id, and CHEMBL25 returns aspirin[30]. ChEMBL’s pChEMBL value is computed only for standard types such as IC50, EC50, Ki and Kd in standard units[3]. Two pitfalls make a right-looking join wrong: matching on the first block alone merges stereoisomers, and adding public activities to training without checking the test scaffolds leaks the test set back in.

    Structure prediction carries its own confidence numbers. AlphaFold’s pLDDT is a per-residue confidence on a 0 to 100 scale: 90 and above is very high, 70 to under 90 confident, 50 to under 70 low, and under 50 very low, which often marks disordered regions[31]. The predicted aligned error (PAE) is the expected position error in ångströms at one residue when the model is aligned on another, capped at 31.75 Å; high PAE between two domains means their relative placement should not be interpreted[31]. The join runs from an internal target id to a UniProt accession, which is 6 or 10 characters[32]. The AlphaFold DB API, queried with that accession, returns a model id such as AF-P00533-F1 and a mean pLDDT, 75.94 for that entry[33]. The mean hides regions: 22.8% of that model’s residues are under 50[33].

    Docking and free-energy perturbation (FEP) are physics-based calculations, not assays. In a benchmark published by the developers of one commercial FEP workflow (FEP+), the median RMSE against experiment was 1.08 kcal/mol; their survey of repeated experimental measurements found a median of 0.85 kcal/mol[34]. A good calculation is still an estimate with an error bar near the assay’s own. So the data model keeps a result-type field (predicted or measured), never overwrites a measurement with a prediction, and stores method and version with each predicted value[34].

    Ontologies and knowledge graphs as join layers

    A knowledge graph stores entities such as targets, diseases and compounds as nodes, and the links between them as edges. Its join keys are identifiers, not names. Open Targets maps all of its evidence to an Ensembl gene id for the target and an EFO id for the disease or phenotype[35]. Ensembl stable ids are meant to keep identifying the same feature across releases, and a version number goes up each time the feature is modified[36]. An ontology is therefore a versioned dependency, like a dictionary. MedDRA is updated twice a year, and users are asked to assess how each change affects their data output[37]. The EMBL-EBI Ontology Lookup Service is a search engine for ontologies, used to annotate data with ontology terms[38]; a BA can use it to check that a term id exists before it goes into a mapping table. Each edge in a graph also needs its evidence source and the release it came from. Open Targets, for example, combines evidence from 20 sources (in its 2021 description) into one scored target-disease association[39], so a score quoted without its release cannot be reproduced. Ontology and graph releases go on the change request like any other configuration item[7].

    Evaluation: the numbers that go into acceptance criteria

    Retrieval metrics need a gold record per query and a stated cut-off k. Recall@k is the share of answerable queries whose gold record appears in the top k. MRR (mean reciprocal rank) averages 1/rank of the first relevant result, with 0 for a miss, and is reported at a cut-off such as MRR@10[21]. nDCG (normalised discounted cumulated gain) sums relevance down the list with a discount for later positions and divides by the ideal ranking[40]. With one relevant record and the common logarithmic discount, 1/log2(rank + 1)[40], rank 1 scores 1, rank 2 scores 0.63 and rank 3 scores 0.5.

    Prediction metrics need the positive rate beside them. AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) is 0.5 for a random model whatever the class balance. On imbalanced data the ROC view can be deceptive, while the precision-recall plot changes with class balance and shows the effect directly[41]. A random model’s PR-AUC sits near the positive fraction[41]. With 5% positives, a PR-AUC of 0.28 is 5.6 times the random baseline, and an AUROC of 0.81 says little on its own. Precision answers the user’s question: of the flagged compounds, how many were positive?

    Calibration is the third property: a calibrated model’s 0.8 is right about 80% of the time. Modern neural networks are often poorly calibrated, and a one-parameter fix called temperature scaling works on most datasets tested[42]. If users act on a probability threshold, calibration on held-out data is an acceptance criterion.

    Human review closes the loop: the plan names which outputs a person reviews, who reviews them and where the judgement is recorded. Each review record must be attributable and traceable, the first and last of the ten ALCOA++ attributes the EMA sets for clinical-trial systems[25].

    GxP: context of use, freezing, change control and who signs

    The FDA draft lays out seven steps: define the question of interest, define the context of use, assess model risk, write a credibility plan, execute it, document results and deviations, and decide adequacy for the context of use[1]. Its scope excludes drug discovery uses, and operational efficiencies that do not affect patient safety, drug quality or study reliability[1]. A discovery QSAR model sits outside that draft; a model feeding a safety, quality or study decision sits inside it.

    The EMA reflection paper was adopted by the CHMP on 9 September 2024 and takes a risk-based approach[27]. It places responsibility on the sponsor for models, datasets and pipelines being fit for purpose, and notes this may be stricter than usual data-science practice[27]. For pivotal trials, the pre-processing pipeline and models are frozen and documented before unblinding, and incremental learning is not accepted[27]. In delivery terms a freeze is a baselined configuration: weights with checksum, code commit, pre-processing, embedding model, index, dictionary versions and thresholds. In January 2026 the EMA and FDA jointly published ten good AI practice principles for drug development. They are high-level principles meant to inform future guidance and standards, and they include a clear context of use and traceable documentation of data, processing steps and analytical decisions[43].

    The document set follows the usual validation path. Annex 15 says requirements should be defined in a URS and/or a functional specification (FS), and that the URS requirements should be verified during design qualification[2]. A design specification (DS) can then describe the build. ICH Q9(R1) asks that the effort, formality and documentation match the level of risk, and lists tools such as FMEA (failure mode and effects analysis)[44]. FDA’s computer software assurance (CSA) guidance, written for medical-device production and quality system software, applies the same risk-based idea to testing[45]. GAMP 5 gives the industry’s risk-based approach for GxP computerised systems[46].

    Change control covers more items in an AI system than in a classic one. Annex 11 requires changes to be made in a controlled manner under a defined procedure[7]. The items include model, embedding model, reranker, index version, chunking rule, prompt templates, thresholds and dictionary versions. MedDRA, for example, releases twice a year: a March X.0 release with changes at all levels and a September X.1 release at the LLT and PT levels only[47]. A version number alone is not enough. On 17 September 2026 the English MedDRA 29.1 files were re-released, because three pairs of new LLTs differ only by a capital or small letter e, and systems that ignore case treated them as duplicates[48]. So a dictionary upgrade needs a load test on the target system, and the change request records which release file was loaded[48]. Each answer the system gives is logged with the query, the retrieved record ids and versions, the model version, the output and the user. 21 CFR 11.10(e) requires secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails that do not obscure earlier entries[49].

    Decide who signs before the project starts. Annex 11 names the process owner, system owner, qualified persons and IT as the roles that cooperate[7]. It names the roles but not the split. One workable RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed): the process owner is accountable for intended use and acceptance criteria, the system owner for configuration, the data scientist runs the evaluation but does not approve their own model’s release, and QA approves plan, report and changes. That separation is this guide’s control design, not a quoted rule. The draft Annex 22, a consultation draft not yet adopted, points the same way: staff who have seen the test data are kept out of training unless they work in pairs[50]. When a vendor supplies the model, the sponsor keeps responsibility, written agreements come before the work, and vendor validation documents can be relied on only after the sponsor has assessed them[25].

    Answerable queries are Q-001, Q-002, Q-003, Q-004 and Q-006; Q-005 is the negative control[data]. Gold ranks are 1, 3, not retrieved, 2 and 1[data].

    • Recall@5 = 4 found / 5 answerable = 80%.
    • MRR@5 = (1/1 + 1/3 + 0 + 1/2 + 1/1) / 5 = 2.833 / 5 = 0.567.
    • nDCG@5 with one relevant record per query = (1 + 0.5 + 0 + 0.631 + 1) / 5 = 3.131 / 5 = 0.626.
    • Negative control: Q-005 abstained, so 1 of 1 passes[data].

    The run fails on recall (80% is below 90%). Two findings go in the test report: Q-003 is a first-stage miss (CASE-2024-0102 never came back, so no reranker can fix it), and Q-004 ranked PROT-001 v2 above the gold v3, which a version filter should prevent[data]. In the messy table, Q-001 has no k, Q-002 has no index version and Q-003 says “found”: none of these can be scored until corrected.

    1. In the good model log, compare EV-01 (random split) and EV-02 (scaffold split). Compute the positive rate, precision and recall for each, and the PR-AUC relative to the random baseline. Which number belongs in the acceptance decision?[data]
    Answer

    Both sets have 100 positives in 2,000 compounds, a 5% positive rate[data].

    • EV-01: precision = 60 / (60 + 140) = 60 / 200 = 30%; recall = 60 / (60 + 40) = 60%; PR-AUC 0.52 / 0.05 = 10.4 times baseline[data].
    • EV-02: precision = 45 / (45 + 205) = 45 / 250 = 18%; recall = 45 / (45 + 55) = 45%; PR-AUC 0.28 / 0.05 = 5.6 times baseline[data].

    The scaffold result goes in the decision, because users will apply the model to new chemical series, not to near-copies of training compounds. The random split’s AUROC of 0.91 against the scaffold’s 0.81 is the size of the optimism[data]. In plain terms for the process owner: on new scaffolds, fewer than 1 in 5 flagged compounds is a true positive.

    1. EV-05 is the part of EV-02 inside the applicability domain. Work out the precision inside and outside the domain, and write one URS line that follows[data].
    Answer

    Inside the domain: precision = 33 / (33 + 77) = 33 / 110 = 30%[data].

    Outside is EV-02 minus EV-05: 2,000 - 1,100 = 900 compounds; positives 100 - 60 = 40; true positives 45 - 33 = 12; false positives 205 - 77 = 128[data]. Precision = 12 / (12 + 128) = 12 / 140 = 8.6%, against a positive rate of 40 / 900 = 4.4%.

    Inside the domain a flag is right 3 times in 10; outside it is barely twice the base rate[data]. A URS line: “The system shall display, with every prediction, whether the compound is inside the applicability domain defined in the model’s specification, and shall report out-of-domain predictions as such.”

    1. EV-04 is the prospective test: compounds made after the model was frozen. Compute precision and recall, and say what a delivery lead should write in the evaluation report about the sample size[data].
    Answer

    The model flagged 3 + 15 = 18 compounds; 3 were positive, so precision = 3 / 18 = 16.7%. Recall = 3 / (3 + 3) = 50%[data].

    The precision is close to the scaffold result (18%), which is reassuring, but the set holds only 6 positives in 120 compounds[data]. One more or one fewer true positive moves recall by 17 percentage points. The report should state the counts, say the prospective estimate is imprecise, and set a review point once a stated number of new positives has accrued. The messy log leaves PR-AUC blank for this row; the report shows it next to the 5% positive rate rather than quoting AUROC alone.

    1. Using the good target table, work out how many residues of each AlphaFold model are under pLDDT 50. Which targets would you pass to the structure team with no further checks? Then name the defect in messy row 2[data].
    Answer

    Residues under 50 = residues modelled × fraction very low[data]:

    • EGFR: 1,210 × 0.228 = 275.9, about 276 residues.
    • TP53: 393 × 0.298 = 117.1, about 117 residues.
    • KRAS: 189 × 0.021 = 4.0, about 4 residues.
    • BRCA1: 1,863 × 0.804 = 1,497.9, about 1,498 residues[data].

    None goes over “with no further checks”. KRAS has a very high mean (91.5) and few low residues, so it is the strongest candidate, but the team still checks per-residue pLDDT at the site of interest[data]. EGFR and TP53 have confident means that hide large low-confidence regions, and PAE is needed before anyone relies on how their regions sit relative to each other. BRCA1’s mean of 41.59 is very low. Messy row 2 stores TP53’s mean as 0.7506, the API value divided by 100, so it was banded “very low” when it is “confident”[data].

    1. Review the messy change control log. How many of the five changes have a defect, which must be reopened before the next release, and who signs each reopened change?[data]
    Answer

    Four of five rows have a defect; only CR-2025-017 (the LIMS patch) is complete[data].

    • CR-2025-014, MedDRA version upgrade: no risk assessment and no tests. Reopen; assess recoding impact, run the regression set; QA and the process owner sign.
    • CR-2025-015, EDC configuration: implemented with no approver. Reopen to record retrospective approval and raise a deviation; data manager and QA sign.
    • CR-2025-016, AI classifier v2.0 to v2.1: “same model family, no re-test”, approved by the data scientist alone. Reopen; re-evaluate on an independent test set; QA and the process owner sign, not the developer.
    • CR-2025-018, retrieval re-index: no impact statement and no gold-set run. Reopen; re-run the retrieval acceptance test against the new index version, recorded in the corpus_version column[data]; QA and the process owner sign.

    Three of the four (014, 016 and 018) are AI or dictionary items, the items classic change control forgets[data].

    2. A classifier tested on 2% positives reports AUROC 0.90 and nothing else. What do you ask for first? a) Training-set accuracy b) A larger model c) PR-AUC with the positive rate, the split used and precision at the operating threshold d) Nothing; 0.90 is high
    3. Under the EMA reflection paper, a model used in a pivotal trial should be: a) Frozen and documented with its pre-processing before unblinding b) Retrained as trial data arrive c) Tuned on the trial’s unblinded data d) Swapped for a newer version if it scores higher
    4. An AlphaFold DB entry has a mean pLDDT of 41.59. Its band is: a) Very high b) Confident c) Low d) Very low
    5. A team joins internal compounds to PubChem on the first 14 characters of the InChIKey. What goes wrong? a) Salts are dropped b) Stereoisomers are merged into one record c) Units change d) Nothing
