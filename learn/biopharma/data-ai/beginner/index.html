    Good data passes five tests before anyone models it

    • Identity and unit: stable id, explicit unit
    • Version: the method version behind each result
    • Provenance and context: audit trail, assay conditions

    • 1,600 nM typed as 5.8 reads 276 times more potent
    • Micro to nano is a factor of 1,000

    Source: EMBL-EBI ChEMBL; US National Institute of Standards and Technology

    Speaker notes

    Learn

    How a medicine reaches a patient, and what each step leaves behind

    The US FDA describes five steps: discovery and development, preclinical research, clinical research, FDA review, and post-market safety monitoring[1]. Discovery starts with a target, a molecule in the body linked to a disease. A project may screen 200,000 to more than 1,000,000 compounds, then narrow hundreds of compounds to one or two candidates[2]. Preclinical work tests toxicity in the lab and in animals before any human dose, under good laboratory practice (GLP, 21 CFR Part 58)[3].

    Before the first human trial a sponsor files an IND (the filing that lets a company start human trials in the US)[4]. In the EU the equivalent is a clinical trial application (CTA); since 31 January 2023 every new one goes through the Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS), and one application can cover up to 30 European countries[5]. Phase I enrols 20 to 100 people, Phase II up to several hundred, and Phase III 300 to 3,000[4].

    A BIO analysis of 9,704 programmes from 2011 to 2020 found that 52.0% passed Phase I, 28.9% passed Phase II, 57.8% passed Phase III and 90.6% of filings were approved, so 7.9% of programmes entering Phase I reached approval[6]. The same analysis puts Phase I to approval at 10.5 years on average[6]. A review of 2010 to 2017 failures attributes 40 to 50% to lack of efficacy[7].

    The filing is an NDA or BLA in the US. The FDA review team has 6 to 10 months to decide on a filed application[8]. In the EU, the CHMP evaluation runs up to 210 active days, and the European Commission decides within 67 days of the opinion[9]. Approved products are made under current good manufacturing practice (21 CFR Parts 210, 211, 212 and 600)[10]. After launch, pharmacovigilance (the science of detecting and preventing adverse effects) continues for the life of the product[11, 12]. Real-world data from health records, claims, registries and digital health devices then shows how the medicine performs in routine care[13].

    Each step writes a different record, in a different system, keyed by a different identifier. Positive outcomes are rare: in the BIO numbers, about 79 of every 1,000 programmes entering Phase I are approved[6].

    Two boundaries catch new engineers. The EU’s CTIS is a submission portal, and EMA states that it is not a clinical trial management system[41]. The EDC holds what sites entered; the SDTM datasets sent to regulators are a later, standardised copy[42, 43].

    Good data in five properties

    Regulators describe good records with ALCOA++: attributable, legible, contemporaneous, original, accurate, complete, consistent, enduring, available and traceable[29]. For an engineer, five tests cover most of it.

    • Identity: each record carries a stable id that never changes meaning, such as a USUBJID that is unique per subject and identical across every SDTM and ADaM (the analysis datasets built from SDTM) dataset in an application[44].
    • Unit: each value carries its unit, because nano is 10 to the power of minus 9 and micro 10 to the power of minus 6, a factor of 1,000[45].
    • Version: each result names the version of the method that produced it, because a specification links each test to an analytical procedure[46].
    • Provenance: each record says who, when and with what, kept in a secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trail[47].
    • Context: each value travels with its conditions, which is why ChEMBL stores activity, assay and target as separate linked records[48].

    A careful team also stores derived values next to raw ones. The IC50 is the concentration of compound that halves the activity of its target in one assay; a lower IC50 means a more potent compound. pIC50 is minus log10 of the molar IC50, so 1 nM is pIC50 9[49].

    Only the company holds its compound registry, ELN entries, batch records, EDC data and safety database. Three rules keep joins honest. Join compounds on the full InChIKey: the first 14 characters hash only the skeleton, so stereoisomers share them[15]. Never turn FAERS (now AEMS) counts into rates: FDA does not receive a report for every event, so incidence cannot be calculated[58]. Never assume a report proves causation, and expect duplicates[60].

    The data and AI lens on day one

    Start every model with a context of use: the specific question it answers and the decision it informs. The FDA’s January 2025 draft guidance sets out seven steps, and defines model risk from model influence and decision consequence[61]. The draft excludes drug discovery uses[61]. EMA expects models and pre-processing for pivotal trials to be frozen and documented before unblinding[62]. In January 2026 FDA and EMA jointly published ten guiding principles of good AI practice in drug development; a clear context of use is one of them[63].

    Document both sides. A model card states intended use and evaluation results across relevant groups[64]. A datasheet states a dataset’s motivation, composition and collection process[65]. Expect imbalance: with 7.9% positives, a model that predicts failure every time is 92.1% accurate and useless[6]. On imbalanced data, precision-recall plots are more informative than ROC plots[66].

    Two mappings appear in week one. OMOP analyses use only Standard Concepts in fields ending in _concept_id, so an unmapped local code drops out of every count[59, 67]. A local code with no vocabulary entry can get its own source concept, numbered above 2,000,000,000; such concepts are never standard, and OHDSI recommends mapping them to a standard concept with a “Maps to” link[68, 69]. Clinical results must trace from analysis back to tabulation and source data; FDA calls this one of the most problematic issues in data conversion[44]. Splits, embeddings, rerankers and judge calibration come in the later levels.

    Example data

    The potency table is a discovery result set. In the messy version, look for a pIC50 typed into the IC50 column, a value entered in µM in a nM column, a missing assay version, a single-concentration result stored as a potency, and a second CPD-0005 row with no replicate id. The good table keeps both CPD-0005 replicates as r1 and r2[data]. Each defect changes the number a model would learn.

    The batch release table comes from a LIMS and the executed batch record. Look for results without a method version or unit, a test with no acceptance criterion, a failing result left unflagged, and a retest written over the original value in the same cell. The good table keeps the failing first run and the retest as two rows, both linked to one investigation id.

    The OMOP table holds diagnoses mapped from health records. The standard concept ids are labelled placeholders (look real ones up in Athena); the local source concept ids sit above 2,000,000,000, the range kept for local use[68]. Look for a concept id of 0, a dropped source value, a text date in a date column, and two patients merged under one person_id. None of these raise an error on load, which is why they survive into analyses.

    Try it

    1. A colleague sends four strings to load as keys: P00533 (a protein), NCT0123456 (a trial), BSYNRYMUTXBXSQ-UHFFFAOYSA-N (a compound) and CHEMBL25 (a compound). Which fails its format check, and why?[19, 56, 15, 48]
      AnswerNCT0123456 fails. An NCT number is NCT followed by 8 digits, and this string has 7[56]. P00533 is 6 characters starting with P, a valid UniProt accession format[19]. The InChIKey is 14 + 1 + 10 + 1 + 1 = 27 characters, as required[15]. CHEMBL25 is a ChEMBL id; the ChEMBL API returns aspirin for it[48]. A format check proves shape only. It does not prove the id points at the record you meant.
    2. In the messy potency table, CPD-0001 shows 7.9 in the IC50 column and no pIC50. The good table shows 12.5 nM and pIC50 7.9. What happened, and what would the same mistake do to CPD-0004?[data]
      AnswerThe pIC50 was typed into the IC50 column[data]. Read as nM, 7.9 nM looks 12.5 / 7.9 = 1.6 times more potent than the true 12.5 nM; its pIC50 would become 9 - log10(7.9) = 8.10 instead of 7.90[49]. CPD-0004 has IC50 1,600 nM and pIC50 5.8[data]. Typed the same way it reads as 5.8 nM: 1,600 / 5.8 = 276 times more potent, and it jumps from weakest to second, behind only CPD-0003 at 3.1 nM[data]. Small numbers in a potency column are the dangerous ones.
    3. The messy potency table has CPD-0005 twice, at 55 and 61 nM, and the second row has no replicate id. Give one summary value, and say why the missing id matters for a join.[data]
      AnswerThe geometric mean is the square root of 55 x 61 = 3,355, which is 57.9 nM[data]. Averaging the pIC50 values gives (7.26 + 7.21) / 2 = 7.235, and 10 to the power of (9 - 7.235) = 58.2 nM; the small gap comes from rounding pIC50 to two decimals. Here the arithmetic mean (58.0 nM) is close, but for replicates of 10 and 1,000 nM it gives 505 nM while the geometric mean gives 100 nM. Without a replicate id nobody can tell a replicate from a duplicate, and a join to another table on compound_id returns two rows for CPD-0005 and doubles its weight. The good table labels them r1 and r2[data].
    4. In the messy batch table, find every result for LOT-2024-008 that is outside its specification, including any that are hidden.[data]
      AnswerWater is 0.62% w/w against a limit of not more than 0.5%, so it is out of specification by 0.12 points, 24% above the limit (0.12 / 0.5)[data]. It is flagged N. The assay cell reads “101.0 (retest, first run 93.8)”[data]. The first run, 93.8% of label claim, is below the 95.0 to 105.0 range, so it is a second OOS result. A retest cannot invalidate the original without a documented laboratory error, so both need investigation[70]. Correctly flagged, the lot has two OOS results, not zero. The good table shows the fix: water flagged Y under INV-2024-0011, and the 93.8% first run and the 101.0% retest as two rows, both under INV-2024-0012[data].
    5. Using the OMOP tables, what share of persons has the standard concept labelled STD-PLACEHOLDER-A in the good version, and in the messy version?[data]
      AnswerGood: persons 1001 and 1002 have STD-PLACEHOLDER-A, out of three distinct persons (1001, 1002, 1003), so 2 / 3 = 66.7%[data]. Messy: row 1 has concept 0, so person 1001 drops out of the numerator but stays in the count of persons; row 4 loads a different patient as 1002, so only two distinct persons remain[data]. That gives 1 / 2 = 50%. Two silent defects moved the answer by 16.7 points. Standard concepts are the only ones analyses use, so unmapped rows vanish[59].
    6. A team trains a model on 1,000 past programmes that entered Phase I, labelled approved or not, at the BIO rate. How many positives are there, how accurate is a model that always says “fails”, and what should the team report instead?[6, 66]
      Answer0.520 x 0.289 x 0.578 x 0.906 = 0.0787, so about 79 approvals and 921 failures[6]. Always saying “fails” is right 921 times: 921 / 1,000 = 92.1% accuracy, with zero approvals found. Report precision and recall instead. Illustrative: a model that flags 100 programmes, 40 of them approved, has precision 40 / 100 = 40% and recall 40 / 79 = 50.6%. Precision-recall plots show this; ROC plots can hide it[66].

    Check yourself

    1. Two stereoisomers of one compound share which part of their InChIKeys? a) The first 14-character block b) Nothing c) The last character only d) The whole key
    2. Why can a count of FAERS reports not give the incidence of a reaction? a) Reports are not coded b) FDA does not receive a report for every event c) The data is private d) It only covers trials
    3. An OMOP condition row has condition_concept_id 0. What does that mean? a) The patient has no condition b) The row was deleted c) The source code was not mapped to a standard concept d) The date is missing
    4. In the FDA’s draft AI credibility framework, model risk combines: a) accuracy and recall b) cost and time c) data size and model size d) model influence and decision consequence
    5. 7.9% of programmes in a dataset are positives. A model predicts “negative” for all. Its accuracy is: a) 7.9% b) 50% c) 92.1% d) 100%

    Answer key: 1-a, 2-b, 3-c, 4-d, 5-c
