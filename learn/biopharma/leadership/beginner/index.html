    How a medicine reaches a patient, what it costs, and where its data is born

    A medicine passes five regulated steps and can take 12 to 15 years

    • Discovery, preclinical, clinical, review, post-market monitoring
    • IND or CTA before the first human dose
    • Phase I 20-100 people; Phase III 300-3,000

    Source: US FDA, 2018; Hughes, Rees, Kalindjian and Philpott, 2011; US FDA, 2018

    Speaker notes

    The FDA decides within 6 to 10 months of a complete NDA or BLA. In the EU the CHMP has up to 210 active days, then the Commission decides within 67 days. For serious conditions the FDA runs four expedited programmes; priority review sets a 6-month goal. EU accelerated assessment cuts 210 days to 150. Since 12 January 2025 new cancer medicines and advanced therapies also face an EU joint clinical assessment. Ask the room: which of these steps does your proposal touch?

    Of 100 programmes entering Phase I, about 8 are approved

    • BIO 2011-2020: 52.0%, 28.9%, 57.8%, 90.6% by step
    • 100 to 52 to 15 to 9 to 8
    • 10.5 years on average from Phase I to approval

    Source: BIO, Informa Pharma Intelligence and QLS Advisors, 2021

Ask: is our forecast using an industry average for a programme that is not average?

    Learn

    How a medicine reaches a patient

    The US FDA describes drug development in five steps: discovery and development, preclinical research, clinical research, FDA review, and post-market safety monitoring[1]. Discovery starts with a target, a molecule in the body linked to a disease. A screen of 200,000 to more than 1,000,000 compounds then finds hits that act on it[2]. Chemists narrow hundreds of compounds to one or two candidates[2]. Preclinical research tests those candidates for toxicity in the laboratory and in animals, under Good Laboratory Practice rules, before any person receives the drug[3].

    Before the first human trial, the company files an IND (Investigational New Drug application) in the US or a CTA (clinical trial application) in the EU[4, 5]. Phase I trials enrol 20 to 100 people and test safety and dose. Phase II enrols up to several hundred patients and asks whether the drug works. Phase III enrols 300 to 3,000 people to confirm the effect and watch for adverse reactions[4]. The company then files an NDA (New Drug Application) or a BLA (Biologics License Application), and the FDA decides within 6 to 10 months of a complete file[6]. In the EU, the CHMP (the EMA’s scientific committee) evaluates for up to 210 active days, and the European Commission decides within 67 days of its opinion[7]. After approval, manufacturing runs under CGMP (current good manufacturing practice) rules. Safety monitoring continues through MedWatch in the US and EudraVigilance in the EU[8, 9, 10]. From idea to launch the whole path can take 12 to 15 years[2].

    Some routes are faster. For serious conditions the FDA runs four expedited programmes: Fast Track, Breakthrough Therapy, Accelerated Approval and Priority Review. Priority review sets a 6-month goal for FDA action[11]. In the EU the CHMP can cut its 210-day evaluation to 150 days under accelerated assessment, for medicines of major interest for public health and therapeutic innovation[12]. Approval is not the last gate. Since 12 January 2025, new cancer medicines and advanced therapies in the EU also go through a joint clinical assessment of their relative effects on health outcomes[13].

    The funnel in numbers, with provenance

    Two public sources give pass rates, and they do not agree, so a careful leader quotes each with its origin. The FDA’s patient pages give rounded figures: about 70% of drugs move on from Phase I, about 33% from Phase II and about 25 to 30% from Phase III[4]. The BIO report on 9,704 programmes from 2011 to 2020 gives 52.0% from Phase I, 28.9% from Phase II, 57.8% from Phase III and 90.6% from filing to approval[14].

    Work it through for 100 programmes entering Phase I, using the BIO rates. 52 reach Phase II. 52 x 0.289 gives about 15 that reach Phase III. 15 x 0.578 gives about 9 that file. 9 x 0.906 gives about 8 approvals. The report’s own figure is a 7.9% likelihood of approval from Phase I[14]. Phase II is the steepest drop in both sets[4, 14]. The same report gives 10.5 years on average from Phase I to approval: 2.3 years in Phase I, 3.6 in Phase II, 3.3 in Phase III and 1.3 in review[14]. It also found that programmes which pre-selected patients with a biomarker had a 15.9% likelihood of approval, about twice the 7.6% of programmes without such biomarkers[14]. These are industry averages across all disease areas; they describe a portfolio, not one programme.

    Why do programmes fail? A review of clinical failures between 2010 and 2017 attributes 40 to 50% to lack of efficacy, about 30% to unmanageable toxicity, 10 to 15% to poor drug-like properties and about 10% to commercial reasons or poor strategy[15]. Most failures are therefore scientific, not operational. That matters when you judge a tool. A tool that speeds up paperwork does not change these shares; a tool that improves the evidence behind a go or stop decision might.

    Cost per approval also depends on the source and the method. A 2011 review gives a round figure of more than $1 billion from idea to launch[2]. A 2024 analysis of US data from 2000 to 2018 gives a mean cash outlay of $172.7 million per approved drug. It rises to $515.8 million once the cost of failed candidates is included, and to $879.3 million once failures and the cost of capital are included, in 2018 dollars[16]. A 2020 study, corrected in 2022, costed 63 of the 355 new drugs and biologics approved in the US between 2009 and 2018, using SEC data. Its base case gives a median of $1,141.7 million and a mean of $1,559.1 million per approval after failures, also in 2018 dollars[17]. The spread is not an error. It reflects whether failures and capital are counted, so quote each number with its method.

    Nine modality classes, one approved example each

    A modality is the class of thing the medicine is. Name the class first and the mechanism second, because the class decides how the medicine is made, tested and regulated. One approved example per class, from the EMA’s public assessment summaries:

    The RNA class also covers siRNA and mRNA medicines; the mRNA example here sits in the vaccine row. Two rows treat the same disease, spinal muscular atrophy, with different classes: an antisense medicine that changes how SMN2 is spliced and a gene therapy that supplies SMN1[28, 24]. The class, not the disease, decides the factory, the release tests and the data the company will hold.

    Each class carries a class risk that a safety section must name. The ADC summary lists low blood counts and raised liver enzymes among common effects[22]. The CAR-T summary reports serious side effects in most patients, including cytokine release syndrome, and treatment centres must stock tocilizumab[23]. Before the AAV gene therapy is given, the label requires AAV9 antibody testing and liver function tests[25].

    Twelve departments and where their data is born

    Each department runs its own system of record, and the data a leader is shown was born in one of them. Discovery works in an ELN (electronic laboratory notebook) and a LIMS (laboratory information management system) that tracks samples, workflows and assay results[30]. Clinical development collects trial data in EDC (electronic data capture), schedules work in a CTMS (clinical trial management system) and files documents in an eTMF (electronic trial master file)[31]. Regulatory affairs assembles the CTD (Common Technical Document) and submits it as eCTD; its five modules are the same across regions except Module 1[32, 33]. Manufacturing records batches in a MES (manufacturing execution system) at ISA-95 Level 3 and plans in an ERP (enterprise resource planning system) at Level 4[34]. Quality runs a pharmaceutical quality system of the kind ICH Q10 describes[35]. Pharmacovigilance keeps a safety database and reports cases to EudraVigilance in the E2B(R3) format[10, 36]. Real-world evidence teams work with health records, claims, registries and digital health data[37]. Supply chain carries serialisation data under DSCSA in the US and the Falsified Medicines Directive in the EU[38, 39]. Medical affairs gives health professionals accurate scientific information through medical science liaisons; this is scientific exchange, not promotion[40]. Market access prepares the evidence that payers and health technology assessment bodies weigh, including the EU joint clinical assessment above[13]. IT and data teams run the shared platforms, and one rule shapes all of them: health data are a special category under the GDPR, and processing them is prohibited unless one of the exceptions in Article 9(2) applies[41]. Commercial completes the twelve. In the US, the holder of an approved drug application sends the FDA each promotional piece for a prescription drug when it is first used, with transmittal Form FDA-2253[42].

    What good data means, in one sentence each

    Five properties separate a table a model can learn from and a table that misleads. Identity: every record carries a stable identifier, such as one USUBJID per trial subject that is identical across every dataset in a submission[43]. Unit: every number carries its unit, because a value in micromolar stored in a nanomolar column is wrong by a factor of 1,000[44]. Version: the assay, dictionary or method version travels with the result, because a MedDRA term or a potency value from a different version is not the same measurement[45, 46]. Provenance: who recorded it, when, on which instrument and under which protocol. The ALCOA++ principles list ten attributes of good records, from attributable, legible and contemporaneous to available and traceable[31]. Context: the conditions of the measurement, such as the ATP concentration of a kinase assay, without which two IC50 values cannot be compared[46]. The UK regulator adds that effort on data integrity should match the risk of a failure, and that shared logins should not be used for regulated data[47].

    Internal versus external data

    Much of what a company needs is public, and each public source has its own identifier. ChEMBL holds measured activities with standard units[46]. PubChem holds compounds that can be looked up by name or structure[48]. UniProt holds annotated protein sequences under accession numbers, and PDB holds experimental structures under four-character identifiers[49, 50]. The AlphaFold database holds predicted structures, keyed by the UniProt accession[51, 52]. Open Targets combines evidence from 20 sources (in its 2021 description) into scored target-disease links[53]. ClinicalTrials.gov registers trials under an NCT number, the letters NCT followed by eight digits[54]. EMA publishes an EPAR for each approved medicine, and the FDA publishes labels[7, 6].

    Safety data needs the most care. AEMS (formerly FAERS), the FDA’s adverse event database, codes events in MedDRA. The FDA says its reports cannot be used to calculate how often an event occurs[55]. Its public dashboard warns that duplicate and incomplete reports are present[56]. EudraVigilance holds suspected adverse reactions for the EU[10]. For health records, DARWIN EU draws on about 40 data partners and about 250 million patients, each converted to the OMOP common data model[57, 58]. A careful team never presents a count of spontaneous reports as a rate.

    What only the company has: its compound registry and ELN, its batch records, its EDC data before publication, its safety cases and its contracts. The value of an AI system in this industry usually lies in joining the two, and the join needs the stable identifiers above.

    Why a model is only as good as its data

    A model trained or grounded on a table inherits that table’s defects. In January 2026 EMA and the FDA published ten joint principles of good AI practice for evidence across the medicines life cycle. They are high-level principles meant to inform future guidance and standards. They ask for a clear context of use and for data provenance and processing steps documented in a traceable way[59]. Older principles from 2021, by the FDA, Health Canada and the MHRA, cover machine learning in medical devices, not drug development; they also keep test data independent of training data[60]. The FDA’s January 2025 draft guidance, which is not binding, asks for test data the model never saw and for maintenance against data drift[61]. Its scope is narrower than many assume: it leaves out drug discovery and operational uses, such as drafting a submission, that do not affect patient safety, drug quality or study reliability[61]. The paper that named retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), where a model reads retrieved documents before it answers, gave provenance as one reason for it[62]. If the documents retrieved are wrong, the answer is wrong with a citation attached.

    The leadership lens on day one

    Three habits. First, ask which department owns the problem and which system holds its data, because each department runs its own systems[30, 31]. Second, tell production evidence from a signal. Merck reports that an internal generative AI platform drafts clinical study reports in 3 to 4 days instead of 2 to 3 weeks; the platform is designed to operate with oversight by qualified medical writers, and the figures are company-reported[63]. The FDA reports that more than 70% of its staff used its Elsa tool voluntarily, per internal data[64]. A partnership announcement valued at up to $1 billion is a signal, not a result[65]. Third, ask for the baseline: who measured the gain, against what, and is it still running.

    Try it

    1. Multiply the four BIO phase-transition rates to reproduce the likelihood of approval from Phase I.[14]
      Answer0.520 x 0.289 x 0.578 x 0.906 = 0.0787, which rounds to 7.9%, the figure the report gives[14]. For 100 programmes: 100 x 0.520 = 52, 52 x 0.289 = 15.0, 15.0 x 0.578 = 8.7, 8.7 x 0.906 = 7.9. The FDA’s rounded rates (about 70%, 33%, 25 to 30%) give a different product, which is why a forecast must name its source[4].
    2. Using the good portfolio table, how many approvals should the five programmes yield on average? Chain the BIO rates from each line’s current phase.[data]
      AnswerPRG-0001 and PRG-0004 are in Phase II: 0.289 x 0.578 x 0.906 = 0.151 each. PRG-0002 is in Phase III: 0.578 x 0.906 = 0.524. PRG-0003 is in Phase I: 0.0787. PRG-0005 is filed: 0.906[data][14]. Sum: 0.151 + 0.151 + 0.524 + 0.079 + 0.906 = 1.81, so about 2 approvals. Say “about 2, on BIO 2011-2020 industry averages”; rates by disease area or with biomarker selection would change it.
    3. In the messy portfolio table, row 1 shows a Phase II pass rate of 33 and no source. What is wrong, and how much does it move that line’s expected value?[data]
      AnswerThe 33 is the FDA’s rounded “about 33%” figure, while the other rows use BIO’s 28.9%[data][4, 14]. With 0.33 the line gives 0.33 x 0.578 x 0.906 = 0.173 instead of 0.151, so mixed sources inflate it by about 14% with no trace of why. The analyst records each rate with its source and uses one source per report.
    4. In the messy potency table, CPD-0002 shows an IC50 of 0.48 in a column whose unit is nM. The good table shows 480 nM. Which is right, and what happens to the ranking if nobody notices?[data]
      Answer0.48 µM equals 480 nM, because micro is 10 to the power of minus 6 and nano is 10 to the power of minus 9[data][44]. The value 0.48 was typed in µM into a nM column. In the good table CPD-0002 ranks fourth of five compounds: CPD-0003 at 3.1 nM, CPD-0001 at 12.5 nM, CPD-0005 at 57.9 nM (the geometric mean of its two replicates, 55 and 61 nM), CPD-0002 at 480 nM and CPD-0004 at 1,600 nM. Left alone, 0.48 makes it the most potent, ahead of CPD-0003 at 3.1 nM, and 1,000 times better than it is.
    5. In the messy potency table, CPD-0001 shows 7.9 in the IC50 column. The good table shows an IC50 of 12.5 nM and a pIC50 of 7.9. What happened, and how large is the error?[data]
      AnswerThe pIC50 was typed into the IC50 column[data]. Read as 7.9 nM, the compound looks about 1.6 times more potent than it is (12.5 / 7.9 = 1.58). The error looks small, which is why it can survive review. The fix is to keep IC50 in nM and pIC50 in its own column[46].
    6. In the messy safety table, which of the four minimum elements is missing in row 1, and what follows?[data]
      AnswerThe patient field is empty, so there is no identifiable patient[data]. A valid individual case safety report needs an identifiable reporter, a single identifiable patient, a suspect reaction and a suspect medicine; lacking any one, the case is incomplete and does not qualify for submission[66]. Row 4 has the same problem with its product, “the blue injection”. The team follows up with the reporter rather than submitting.
    7. Write one sentence on cost per approval that a board could not fault.[16]
      AnswerOne acceptable form: “Estimates of cost per approved drug range from $879.3 million (US 2000-2018 data, including failures and cost of capital, 2018 dollars) to a median of $1,141.7 million and a mean of $1,559.1 million (63 US approvals 2009-2018, SEC data, 2018 dollars, as corrected in 2022), beside an older round figure of more than $1 billion”[16, 17, 2]. The sentence names the method behind each number.
