Biopharma for Leadership and partners

The industry in one hour: economics, rules, and where AI is in real use

Most drugs fail in Phase II, so a pipeline is a portfolio of likely stops

  • Idea to launch: 12-15 years and over $1 billion
  • Of 100 drugs entering Phase I, about 8 are approved
  • Phase II is the biggest drop: only 28.9% advance

Source: Hughes, Rees, Kalindjian and Philpott, 2011; BIO, Informa Pharma Intelligence and QLS Advisors, 2021

Speaker notes

The clinical figures come from 9,704 programmes tracked from 2011 to 2020. Phase II failures come after years of spend; Phase I loses drugs earlier, in trials of 20 to 100 people. Ask a client how many of its programmes are in Phase II today.

Most failures are scientific, so ask whether a tool improves the evidence

  • The drug does not work: 40-50% of failures
  • Unmanageable side effects: about 30%
  • Commercial or strategy reasons: about 10%

Source: Sun, Gao, Hu and Zhou, 2022

Speaker notes

These shares come from a 2022 review of clinical failures between 2010 and 2017. Poor drug-like properties explain another 10-15%. Ask of any proposal: does it touch efficacy, safety, or neither?

Approval runs on fixed clocks, so filings set a client's calendar

  • US: FDA decides 6 to 10 months after a complete file
  • EU: CHMP evaluates in up to 210 active days
  • Four faster US routes exist for serious conditions

Source: US FDA, 2018; European Medicines Agency; US FDA, 2023

Speaker notes

The 210 days do not count clock stops, when the company answers questions. The European Commission then decides within 67 days of the CHMP opinion. Priority Review aims for action within 6 months; Accelerated Approval can rest on a surrogate endpoint. Ask which of the client's drugs hold an expedited designation: it changes their timeline.

Approval is not access, so plan for payers' assessments too

  • EU joint clinical assessments since 12 January 2025
  • First in scope: new cancer medicines and advanced therapies
  • Generics follow when protection ends, via an ANDA

Source: European Commission; US FDA, 2018

Speaker notes

A joint clinical assessment compares a medicine's effects with other options and supports each member state's own health technology assessment. Generics usually rely on bioequivalence studies, not new clinical trials. Ask which upcoming assessments set the client's deadlines.

Each department runs its own systems, so find the owner before the fix

  • Lab, trials, regulatory, plant and safety each run own systems
  • Data moves between them in set standards
  • Systems used in GMP work must be validated

Source: Raviteja and Gupta, JSS College of Pharmacy, 2013; European Medicines Agency (GCP IWG), 2023; US FDA, 2026; ISA; European Medicines Agency, 2020; European Commission, 2011

Speaker notes

Trial data, for example, moves from collection (CDASH) to tabulation (SDTM) to analysis (ADaM), then into a clinical study report. Each department owns its own systems, budget and problems. Ask which department owns the problem before proposing a fix.

Every change to a validated system costs time, so regulation sets the pace

  • Validation effort follows a documented risk assessment
  • Every change goes through a defined change-control procedure
  • Suppliers accept their responsibilities in a written agreement

Source: European Commission, 2011; ISPE, 2022

Speaker notes

EU Annex 11 sets these rules for computer systems in manufacturing. In the US, Part 11 covers electronic records and signatures kept under FDA rules. GAMP 5 is the industry's risk-based method. Ask a client how long its last validated release took.

AI is in use for drafting reports and helping FDA staff

  • Merck: report first drafts in 3-4 days, not 2-3 weeks
  • Signal only: a Bayer study, one team of 3
  • FDA: more than 70% of staff used Elsa voluntarily

Source: Merck & Co., 2025; Benaiche et al. (Bayer Healthcare SAS France), 2025; US FDA, 2025

Speaker notes

Merck's platform is designed to operate with oversight by qualified medical writers; its figures are company-reported. The Bayer figure, a signal rather than production evidence, is from a peer-reviewed study of about four months in a team of 3, drafting case documentation letters. The FDA figure is internal data; Novo Nordisk and Sanofi figures are vendor-published. Ask a vendor: who measured the gain, and against what baseline?

Announcements are signals, not results, so ask what evidence fits the use

  • Merck and Google Cloud: agentic AI, up to $1 billion
  • Chai Discovery: 2026 deals with Lilly, Pfizer, Novartis, others
  • FDA draft guidance (January 2025): credibility judged per use

Source: Merck & Co., 2026; Chai Discovery; US FDA, 2025

Speaker notes

Signal, not yet shown in production: these are 2026 announcements, not evidence of results. The FDA guidance is a draft and not binding; EMA's 2024 reflection paper covers AI across a medicine's life cycle. A tool that drafts an internal email is not judged like one that shapes a safety decision. Treat a signal as a reason to ask, not as proof.

Biopharma on one page, for Leadership and partners

The chain, ten terms, five questions, cases.

The chain in one line

Discovery → preclinical research → clinical trials → regulatory review → post-market safety monitoring[1]. Idea to launch takes 12-15 years and over $1 billion[2]. Of 100 drugs that start Phase I, about 8 are approved, on average 10.5 years later[3].

Ten terms

  • Phase I-III: trials in 20-100, up to several hundred, then 300-3,000 people[4].
  • IND: the US filing needed before the first trial in people[4].
  • NDA: the US New Drug Application; the FDA decides within 6-10 months of a complete file[5].
  • Expedited programmes: Fast Track, Breakthrough Therapy, Accelerated Approval, Priority Review (6-month goal)[6].
  • CHMP opinion: EMA’s evaluation, up to 210 active days; the European Commission then decides[7].
  • Joint clinical assessment: EU comparison of clinical evidence; since 12 January 2025 for new cancer medicines and advanced therapies[8].
  • ANDA: the abbreviated US route for generics, usually based on bioequivalence, not new trials[9].
  • GxP validation: systems used in manufacturing are validated; every change goes through change control[10].
  • Production AI: in use at a named organisation, shown by a public source, not just announced.
  • Signal: a deal, licence or launch not yet shown in production.

Five questions to ask

  1. Which programmes carry an expedited designation, and what is the filing timeline?
  2. Which department owns this problem, and which systems hold its data?
  3. Is the work GxP-regulated? Who validates changes, and who signs off?
  4. For any AI claim: against what baseline, measured by whom, still running?
  5. Who reviews the AI output, and where is that review recorded?

Cases and one signal

  • Merck & Co. drafts clinical study reports with generative AI, with oversight by qualified medical writers; first drafts in 3-4 days instead of 2-3 weeks (company-reported)[11].
  • Signal, not yet shown in production: Bayer. One French drug-safety team saved 23.3% of time per case in a peer-reviewed study of about four months, and kept using the tool[12].
  • US FDA: more than 70% of staff used its Elsa tool voluntarily, per FDA internal data in a December 2025 release[13].
