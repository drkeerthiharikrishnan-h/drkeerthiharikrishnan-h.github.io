    Learn

    Start with the decision, the label and the positives

    Most AI proposals describe a model. The questions that decide whether it will work come earlier: which decision the output feeds, what counts as a correct answer, and how many correct examples exist. The FDA’s draft credibility framework starts in the same place. Its first two steps are the question of interest and the context of use (COU), meaning the specific role and scope of the model in that decision[1].

    A label is the recorded outcome a model learns to predict. In discovery it is often a potency cut-off: a compound counts as “active” if its IC50 (the concentration that halves the target’s activity) is under 1 µM in one named assay. ChEMBL stores such results in standard units with a pChEMBL value, the negative log of the molar potency, so 1 nM is 9 and 1 µM is 6[2]. The OECD lists “a defined endpoint” first among its principles for a predictive model used in regulation[3]. A label built from two assays run under different conditions is really two labels. Ask for the assay id and version behind every label.

    The funnel makes positive labels scarce. A discovery project can screen 200,000 to more than 1,000,000 compounds to reach one or two candidates[4]. Of programmes that entered Phase I from 2011 to 2020, 7.9% reached approval, and Phase II had the lowest transition rate, 28.9%[5]. Lack of clinical efficacy explains 40-50% of clinical failures[6]. A model that predicts programme success therefore learns from a few positives per hundred programmes. The MoleculeNet benchmark authors found that learned molecular representations still struggle with data scarcity and highly imbalanced classification[7].

    The beginner module gave the cost of an approval. Here the point is where the money goes. Phase 3 is the largest share of clinical cost[8]. In the same cost model, a Phase 3 trial enrols about 630 patients on average against 51 in Phase 1, and runs 38.0 months against 27.8[8]. A failure found in Phase 3 has already paid for that. An AI system earns a budget line when it changes a decision that makes failure earlier or cheaper, and the evaluation must measure that decision.

    Data readiness is a separate question from data volume. The FAIR principles ask that data be findable, accessible, interoperable and reusable by machines, not only by people[9]. In practice that means each record carries a stable identifier, a unit, an assay or method version and a date, and that its joins to public sources work. A team that cannot produce these fields for its own data is not ready to train on it.

    Embeddings: text, molecules and proteins as numbers

    An embedding is a list of numbers (a vector) that places an item so that similar items sit close together. Sentence-BERT showed why this matters for search. Finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences took about 65 hours when a BERT model compared every pair, and about 5 seconds with sentence embeddings compared by cosine similarity[10]. No text-embedding model wins everywhere: the MTEB benchmark tested 33 models on 58 datasets and found none dominant across all tasks[11]. Choose the embedding model on your own documents, not from a leaderboard.

    Documents are cut into chunks before they are embedded. The common method splits on a fixed number of tokens, such as 100, 256 or 512; larger chunks carry more context but also more noise and cost[12]. Life-science documents have structure that a fixed cut ignores. A clinical study report (CSR) follows the ICH E3 layout, which is the same core report in every ICH region[13]. A fixed cut can separate a number in a CSR table from its unit, or an inclusion criterion in a protocol from its exception. Chunk on the document’s own sections and store metadata with every chunk: document id, version, section and effective date[12].

    Molecules have their own vectors. Extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP) record which small circular substructures a molecule contains; they were built for structure-activity modelling and come from a variant of the Morgan algorithm[14]. Fingerprints are fast and readable, because each feature is a substructure[14]. They also explain a common leak: close analogues have similar fingerprints, so a random split puts near-twins on both sides of the test line.

    Proteins have language-model embeddings. ESM-2 learns from sequences alone; as the models were scaled to 15 billion parameters, an atomic-level picture of structure emerged in their learned representations, and ESMFold reads structure from them[15]. Teams use such vectors to compare, cluster or predict properties of proteins without an experimental structure.

    Vectors live in an index. Many vector databases use approximate nearest-neighbour search such as HNSW, a layered graph that finds close vectors without comparing every one[16]. Approximate means it can miss a close match. Measure recall on your own corpus after every re-index.

    Retrieval and reranking: find the right record first

    Lexical retrieval, of which BM25 is the standard example, scores documents by shared words. It finds exact identifiers, codes and product names. Dense retrieval compares embeddings and finds paraphrases. Across 18 public datasets, BM25 was a robust baseline; dense models were cheaper than rerankers but often underperformed[17]. Sparse and dense methods capture different signals and can be combined, which is called hybrid retrieval[12]. In this industry the exact token often decides the answer. An NCT number is “NCT” plus 8 digits[18]; a dense model can rank a neighbouring trial above the one the user typed.

    Filters on controlled vocabularies narrow the search before ranking. MedDRA, the safety dictionary, has five levels, from 27 system organ classes down to lowest level terms[19]. In the OMOP common data model, only standard concepts fill fields ending in _CONCEPT_ID, and several source codes for one condition map to one standard concept[20]. A filter on the standard concept finds records coded from different source vocabularies; a filter on one source code misses the rest. The filter values carry a dictionary version, and a dictionary upgrade is a change to control.

    Retrieval returns a top k, the first k results. A reranker then reads the query and each candidate together and re-orders the list. A BERT cross-encoder used this way reached the top of the MS MARCO passage leaderboard, a 27% relative gain in MRR at 10 over the previous best[21]. The cost is that every query-document pair passes through the network, which raises compute by orders of magnitude[22]. So rerankers re-order a short list, not the whole corpus. A reranker cannot recover a record that retrieval never returned. When the right SOP sits outside the short list, it stays lost.

    Some systems retrieve over a knowledge graph rather than over text: a store of entities, such as targets, diseases and compounds, linked by evidence. Open Targets is a public example; it integrates evidence from 20 sources (in its 2021 description) into scored target-disease associations[23]. Such a graph holds together only if every source uses the same identifier for the same entity, so identifiers are its join keys. Ontologies supply the shared terms. EMBL-EBI’s Ontology Lookup Service is a search engine for the ontologies used to annotate biomedical data[24]. In OMOP, source codes are mapped to standard concepts through the vocabulary tables[20]. Treat the ontology and vocabulary versions as inputs under change control, like the model version: a new release can move a mapping, and every answer that used it.

    Every retrieved item needs a locator: document id, version and section, or table and row. EMA expects a trial master file to support document identification, version history, search and retrieval[25]. A locator is how a reviewer goes from an AI answer back to the governed record.

    Grounded generation: answer from the record, or say there is none

    Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pairs a language model with a retriever over a document index. Its authors named provenance and updating knowledge as problems for models that rely on their parameters alone[26]. Grounding means the answer uses only retrieved text and cites a locator for each claim. RAG evaluation now scores separate abilities; one is negative rejection, declining to answer when the retrieved text does not contain the answer[12]. In a regulated setting “no record found” is a correct answer. A fluent answer with no record behind it is the most expensive failure.

    Scoring answers is itself a model problem. Frameworks such as Ragas score retrieval relevance, faithfulness to the retrieved context and answer quality separately, without human labels[27]. Strong LLM judges reached over 80% agreement with human preferences but show position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases[28]. Use a model judge only after checking it against expert grades on a sample. Never let a model grade its own answers.

    A measured result looks like this. In a published study of Bayer’s internal assistant, time spent drafting pharmacovigilance case documentation letters fell by 23.3% (95% CI 13.8-32.8%)[29]. The study followed one team of 3 for 4 months: 2 months before the tool and 2 months after it[29]. Note the interval, the narrow task, the size and the design. A before-and-after comparison in one small team is a signal worth testing, not a forecast for a whole department.

    Predictions in discovery: properties, structures and poses

    QSAR (quantitative structure-activity relationship) models predict a measured property from structure; ADMET models are QSAR models for absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity. The OECD principles ask for a defined endpoint, an unambiguous algorithm, a defined applicability domain, measures of fit, robustness and predictivity, and a mechanistic interpretation if possible[3]. The applicability domain exists because such models are reductionist: they predict reliably only for the structures, properties and mechanisms they were built on[3]. Ask what share of the compounds you care about sits inside it.

    AlphaFold predicts a protein’s 3D structure from its sequence. In the CASP14 assessment its median backbone error was 0.96 Å against 2.8 Å for the next best method[30]. Each prediction carries two confidence measures. pLDDT is per residue, on a 0 to 100 scale: 90 and above is very high, 70 to 90 confident, 50 to 70 low, under 50 very low, and very low often marks disorder[31]. PAE (predicted aligned error) is per pair of residues, in Å: the expected error in one residue’s position when the structures are aligned on another. It shows how confident AlphaFold is that two domains are placed correctly relative to each other[32]. Both are confidence estimates, not measurements. Two well-predicted domains can sit at an uncertain angle to each other.

    Docking places a small molecule into a protein pocket and scores the pose. An assessment of 10 docking programs and 37 scoring functions found that docking could pick actives out of decoys, though no program worked for every target. None of the scoring functions made a useful prediction of binding affinity[33]. Free energy perturbation (FEP) simulates the change in binding between two related compounds. An error of 1 kcal/mol can mean a wrong prediction of up to a 5-fold difference in binding affinity[34]. Treat the next figures as developer-published. A 2023 retrospective benchmark from Schrödinger, the company that sells FEP software, put the method’s median root-mean-square error against experiment at 1.08 kcal/mol across 1,237 ligands. Its survey of affinities measured by more than one experimental method found a median of 0.85 kcal/mol[35]. The useful question is how close a prediction comes to the assay’s own repeatability. Docking scores and FEP values are predictions to be tested in an assay, not results.

    Useful predictions join internal records to public ones. Take compound CPD-0001 from the registry, standardise it to the parent structure (salts stripped) and compute its InChIKey. PubChem can be queried by InChIKey; ChEMBL records are fetched by ChEMBL id once the structure is resolved[36, 37]. Match on the full 27-character key for the same stereoisomer. The first 14-character block encodes the skeleton only, so a block match finds the same skeleton with other stereochemistry and must be labelled as such[38]. For the target, use the UniProt accession: P00533 is EGFR, a 1,210-residue sequence[39]. The AlphaFold database returns model AF-P00533-F1 for it, with mean pLDDT 75.94[40]. Open Targets then gives scored target-disease associations for the same target[41, 23].

    What a careful team never does: compare a public pChEMBL value with an internal IC50 without checking the assay type and units[2]; quote a docking score as an affinity[33]; or treat an AlphaFold model as an experimental structure[31].

    Evaluation is the decision instrument

    A test set means something only if it resembles the future. A random split puts close analogues on both sides. Scaffold splitting groups compounds by their 2D ring framework (the Bemis-Murcko scaffold) and is harder for models than a random split; time splitting trains on older compounds and tests on newer ones[7]. In a QSAR study, a time split gave an R² closer to true prospective performance than random selection, which was too optimistic[42]. The FDA draft asks that test data be independent of development data and never used in training[1]. Leakage also enters through duplicates: one compound registered twice, a salt form, or a re-test.

    Retrieval has its own measures. Recall at k is the share of queries whose correct record appears in the top k. MRR (mean reciprocal rank) averages 1 divided by the rank of the correct record. nDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain) gives more credit to correct records ranked higher. Hit rate, MRR and nDCG are the usual retrieval metrics for RAG[12]. Each must state its k and its gold set version[17].

    Predictions need two kinds of metric. AUROC (area under the ROC curve) measures how well a model ranks positives above negatives, and it does not change with class balance. PR-AUC (area under the precision-recall curve) does change with class balance, and on imbalanced data it is the more informative plot[43]. A random guess scores a PR-AUC equal to the positive rate: with 20 actives in 400 compounds, 0.05[43]. Calibration asks whether a predicted 0.3 means that about 30% of such compounds are active. Modern neural networks are poorly calibrated, and temperature scaling is a simple correction[44].

    Two more checks complete an evaluation. First, a simple baseline, such as nearest-neighbour similarity on the same test set. Second, expert review of a sample, with the grade recorded. The 2021 good machine learning practice principles were written for medical devices; they ask for multidisciplinary expertise across the life cycle and for monitoring of deployed models[45]. For drug development, EMA and FDA published ten joint principles of good AI practice in January 2026. They are high-level principles meant to inform future guidance and standards, and they include a clear context of use, multidisciplinary expertise, and documented data governance in line with GxP[46].

    The same discipline separates proven from signalled. An analysis of AI-native biotech pipelines found 80-90% Phase I success for AI-discovered molecules, higher than historic rates, and about 40% in Phase II on a limited sample, comparable to historic rates; the authors call these early signs[47]. The industry benchmark is 52.0% for Phase I and 28.9% for Phase II across 9,704 programmes[5]. Phase I studies mainly safety and dosage[48]. A fair reading: AI design shows promise on drug-like properties, and the efficacy question, the main cause of failure, is still open.

    GxP: when a model output becomes a regulated record

    The FDA’s January 2025 draft guidance is not binding. It sets out a seven-step, risk-based credibility process: define the question, define the context of use, assess model risk, plan, execute, document results and deviations, and decide whether the model is adequate for that context[1]. Model risk combines model influence, how much the decision rests on the model, with decision consequence, the harm of a wrong call[1]. The draft leaves out drug discovery and operational uses that do not affect patient safety, drug quality or study reliability[1].

    The EMA reflection paper was adopted in September 2024. For pivotal trials it expects the pre-processing pipeline and models to be frozen and documented before unblinding, and it does not accept incremental learning[49]. A hold-out test set is used once; after further development, a new independent test set is needed[49]. The sponsor is responsible for fitness for purpose under GxP, which may be stricter than common data-science practice[49]. The freeze and the ban on incremental learning are scoped to pivotal trials. In pharmacovigilance the paper allows a more flexible approach: incremental learning may keep improving models that classify adverse event reports or score their severity, as long as performance is monitored and documented[49]. So a case-intake classifier may learn in service, but each version that goes live is still recorded, evaluated and approved, as the example change log below shows[data].

    In a validated system, a new model version, a re-indexed corpus, a new embedding model or a MedDRA upgrade is a change. MedDRA is released twice a year: the March X.0 release can change all levels, the September X.1 release only the lowest level terms and preferred terms, and users should assess the impact of each change on their data output[50, 51]. EU Annex 11 applies to computerised systems in GMP activities and expects validated applications and controlled change[52]. Under 21 CFR 11.10(e), audit trails must be secure, computer-generated and time-stamped, and record changes must not obscure earlier entries[53]. For an AI output, that trail should hold the model version, index version, prompt template, retrieved locators and the human reviewer.

    The leadership lens: five questions and the build, buy or partner choice

    The five questions from the intermediate module still apply. At this level each one is sharpened by what you now know about labels, splits and metrics. They follow the order of the credibility framework: decision first, then evidence, then life cycle[1, 49].

    1. Which decision, department and system of record? Sharpened: what is the label that stands for a right answer, and how many positives are in the data we hold?
    2. Which identifiers join the records, and what share of rows join on every key? Sharpened: how many test records also sit in training under another key, such as a salt form or a re-test?
    3. What is the evaluation set? Sharpened: which split method and training cut-off date, against what baseline, on which metric, at what operating point (k, threshold, positive rate)?
    4. What is the context of use and the cost of a wrong answer? Sharpened: what happens when the model is wrong or has no record: abstention or human review?
    5. Who keeps it valid when an assay version, a method or a MedDRA version changes? Sharpened: which changes, including a new model, embedding model or re-indexed corpus, trigger change control?

    Public announcements show three patterns. Some companies build internal assistants and measure them, as in the pharmacovigilance study above[29]. Some license access to an external model maker; one such maker lists six pharma partnerships dated January to October 2026, several with terms not disclosed[54, 55, 56, 57]. Some partner with peers: five companies jointly fine-tuned an open structure-prediction model on more than 20,000 private structures without pooling them, according to the platform company that ran the network[58]. The choice turns on who owns the data, who owns the evaluation and who carries change control. None of the three removes the need for your own frozen test set.

    Try it

    1. A partner proposes to predict approval from Phase I data and holds 300 programmes that entered Phase I. Using the industry rate, how many positive labels should you expect, and how many in a 20% test set?[5]
    Answer

    Likelihood of approval from Phase I is 7.9%[5]. Whole dataset: 300 × 0.079 = 23.7, so about 24 approvals. A 20% test set holds 60 programmes: 60 × 0.079 = 4.7, so about 5 positives. One missed positive moves recall by 1 / 5 = 20 percentage points. Ask for confidence intervals and for a definition of “approval” (first approval of the molecule, or any indication).

    1. In the good evaluation log, compute the positive rate and the precision at threshold 0.5 for the scaffold run (RUN-02), the time run (RUN-03) and the baseline (RUN-04). Which number belongs in the business case?[data]
    Answer

    RUN-02: positive rate 20 / 400 = 0.05; PR-AUC 0.24 is 0.24 / 0.05 = 4.8 times the chance level; precision 9 / 35 = 0.26[data]. RUN-03: positive rate 15 / 380 = 0.039; precision 6 / 33 = 0.18, recall 6 / 15 = 0.40[data]. RUN-04 baseline: precision 5 / 30 = 0.17, recall 5 / 15 = 0.33[data]. The time-split numbers belong in the business case, because they come closest to future use. Against the baseline the model finds one more active (6 against 5) out of 15, and 27 of its 33 flags are false alarms. The prospective run (RUN-05) found 1 of 3 actives with 8 flags; 3 actives are too few to conclude anything.

    1. In the messy evaluation log, what share of the RUN-01 test set was also in training, and how much did the leak and the late training cut-off inflate AUROC?[data]
    Answer

    RUN-01: 37 / 400 = 0.0925, so 9.25% of test compounds were in training; AUROC reads 0.95 against 0.91 in the clean run, and PR-AUC 0.68 against 0.52[data]. RUN-03: the training cut-off of 2025-06-30 is after the first test registrations on 2025-01-01, so it is not a time split; AUROC reads 0.86 against 0.74 when the cut-off is 2024-12-31[data]. The fix is to group by InChIKey before splitting and to freeze the cut-off before the test period. For leakage control the group can deliberately use the first InChIKey block, so stereoisomers count as near-duplicates; joins still match the full key[38]. Note also the version in the clean log: the model retrained on the 2024-12-31 snapshot is labelled ACT-CLS v1.3-T2024, because a model retrained on new data is a new version, even with the same code[data].

    1. From the good retrieval log, compute recall at 5 and MRR. Then compute the MRR the messy log reports and say why it cannot be right.
    Answer

    Good log: gold ranks 1, 3, none, 2, 1. Recall at 5 = 4 / 5 = 0.80. MRR = (1.0 + 0.33 + 0 + 0.5 + 1.0) / 5 = 2.83 / 5 = 0.57[data]. Messy log as recorded: (1.0 + 0.14 + 1.0 + 2 + 1.0) / 5 = 5.14 / 5 = 1.03[data]. A reciprocal rank can never exceed 1, so an MRR above 1 proves a typing error (row 4 holds the rank, 2, not 1 / 2 = 0.5). Row 2 also claims a hit at rank 7 with k = 5, row 3 has no gold document and a self-judging model, and row 5 used gold set v1; drop or redo those rows before reporting anything.

    1. A partner says its target model is reliable for docking because AF-P00533-F1 has mean pLDDT 76. The AlphaFold database gives the fractions of residues per band as 0.228 very low, 0.065 low, 0.233 confident and 0.474 very high. What share is at 70 or above, and roughly how many residues are very low?[40]
    Answer

    At 70 or above: 0.233 + 0.474 = 0.707, about 71%[40]. Very low: the model covers residues 1 to 1,210, the full sequence, so 0.228 × 1,210 = 276 residues[40, 39]. A mean hides this spread. Ask for pLDDT at the pocket residues and PAE between the domains that form the site[32]. Even then the docking score ranks poses and does not predict affinity[33].

    1. In the messy change control log, how many of the five changes reached “implemented” without approval, and which rows changed an AI component without a re-test?
    Answer

    Three of five rows (CR-2025-015, CR-2025-016, CR-2025-018) are “implemented” rather than approved[data]. Two AI components changed without a re-test: the case-intake classifier went from v2.0 to v2.1 with no tests and only the data scientist’s approval, and the retrieval index was re-indexed with no impact statement and no gold-set re-run[data]. Only CR-2025-017 is complete; CR-2025-014 is approved but has no risk assessment or tests for a MedDRA upgrade. That is 1 of 5 changes fully controlled.

    Check yourself

    1. A dense retriever ranks a neighbouring trial above the NCT number the user typed. What should be fixed first? a) Use a larger generator model b) Add lexical (BM25) retrieval and an exact filter on the NCT field c) Make chunks longer d) Lower the reranker’s cost
    2. A classifier reports AUROC 0.90 on a label with 2% positives. What else must you see before deciding? a) Training loss b) Parameter count c) PR-AUC with the positive rate, and precision at the chosen threshold d) A larger AUROC
    3. An AlphaFold model shows high pLDDT within two domains and high PAE between them. What can you conclude? a) Each domain is likely well predicted, but their relative placement is uncertain b) Both domains and their arrangement are reliable c) The protein is fully disordered d) The model is an experimental structure
    4. Under the EMA reflection paper, when must the model and pre-processing pipeline for a pivotal trial analysis be frozen? a) After unblinding b) At submission c) Never, because incremental learning is preferred d) Before unblinding, with documentation
    5. A vendor reports AUROC 0.95 on a random split, with 37 test compounds whose InChIKeys also appear in training. What is the best response? a) Accept the result b) Ask for a group split by InChIKey and a time split, with a nearest-neighbour baseline c) Ask for more training epochs d) Ask for docking scores instead

    Answer key: 1-b, 2-c, 3-a, 4-d, 5-b
