    What you will be able to do

    • Commission an evaluation bank that can tell two AI systems apart on your own records, with answer keys, locators and negative controls.
    • Read an evaluation report and say whether the system is fit for one named use, starting from the fabrication rate on negative controls, and turn a published time saving into a planning range with a kill criterion.
    • Set the governance after go-live: who re-runs the bank, when a model or dictionary change needs change control, and what goes in the file a regulator reads.

    Learn

    The decision you own: is this system good enough for this use?

    At this level you are not the person who builds the system. You are the person who signs that it may be used. That signature carries weight because the decisions downstream are expensive. The beginner module gave the cost figures; the point here is that failed programmes are a major part of what each approved drug costs[1]. A tool that feeds a Phase II go/no-go, a batch release or a safety assessment is therefore a decision input, not an office convenience.

    Regulators frame the question the same way. The FDA’s January 2025 draft guidance asks whether a model is credible for a specific context of use, meaning the exact question the model answers and the role its output plays in a decision[2]. It defines model risk as the combination of model influence (how much the output drives the decision) and decision consequence (how bad a wrong decision would be)[2]. The draft is not for implementation yet, and it leaves out drug discovery and efficiency uses that do not affect patient safety, drug quality or study reliability[2]. The EMA reflection paper, adopted in September 2024, takes a risk-based approach with its own categories. It uses “high patient risk” for systems that affect patient safety, and “high regulatory impact” where the effect on regulatory decisions is substantial[3]. ICH Q9(R1) adds the general rule: effort, formality and documentation should match the level of risk[4].

    The instrument that turns this into a decision is an evaluation bank. A bank is a fixed set of questions, each with an answer key written from the source before the system runs. Published case studies cannot replace it. Merck reports that its internal platform cut first drafts of clinical study reports from 2-3 weeks to 3-4 days, a company-reported figure from a system in use[5]. A peer-reviewed Bayer study found a 23.3% time saving drafting safety case documentation (95% CI 13.8-32.8%), in one team of 3 over 4 months, comparing 2 months before the tool with 2 months after[6]. The FDA says more than 70% of its staff used its internal assistant voluntarily, which measures adoption, not accuracy[7]. None of these numbers tells you how a system performs on your compounds, your lots and your trials.

    Four knowledge layers, and what each failure tells you

    Every item in a bank is tagged with the layer of knowledge that should answer it. Layer P (parametric) is what the model learned in training and stores in its weights. Lewis and colleagues showed that such parametric memory struggles to give precise facts, provenance and updated knowledge[8]. Layer S (source-grounded) is the document or table you hand the system with the question. Layer I (internal records) is what sits in your own systems, such as the electronic lab notebook (ELN), the laboratory information management system (LIMS) or the safety database. Layer L (literature and public databases) is published work and resources such as ChEMBL or ClinicalTrials.gov. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is the common design that adds a document index to the model, so it can read S, I and L at question time[8].

    The layer tags turn a score into a diagnosis. If the system passes P items and fails S items, the model is fine and the retrieval is broken. If it fails P items, the problem is the model itself. If it answers an S or I question with a plausible public value, it has substituted knowledge it never read. Items that need two layers at once test integration, which is usually what you are paying for. The Ragas authors make a related point for RAG systems: retrieval quality, faithful use of the retrieved text and the quality of the answer are separate things and should be measured separately[9].

    The most valuable items sit where internal records meet a public source, because that is where joins fail. A registered compound joins to ChEMBL through its Standard InChIKey, a fixed 27-character hash whose first 14-character block encodes the skeleton and whose second block encodes stereochemistry and isotopes[10]. A match on the first block alone can join your compound to its mirror-image isomer. ChEMBL then addresses molecules, assays and activities by ChEMBL id[11], and reports activities in standardised units[12]. A target joins to UniProt by accession[13]; a trial joins to its registry entry by NCT number[14]. An adverse event is coded to a MedDRA Preferred Term, one of five levels in its hierarchy[15]. In OMOP, the common data model for real-world data, only Standard Concepts may sit in fields ending in _CONCEPT_ID, and local source codes are mapped to them[16]. Each of these joins deserves at least one item.

    Items that discriminate: type, weight, swap test and chain

    Each item also carries a question type: factual, retrieval, quantitative, reasoning, comparative, integration, troubleshooting, prioritisation, hypothesis, protocol, exploratory or abstention. The type sets the scoring weights, so the weights move with the question. A lookup is scored mostly on correctness and on whether the answer points to the record it came from. A hypothesis has no single key, so it is scored on the reasoning, on whether the test could be run, and on grounding that keeps it from becoming free association. An abstention item is scored almost entirely on whether the system declined to invent an answer. In this guide’s method, each answer is graded 0, 1 or 2 on up to nine dimensions: correctness, completeness, grounding, source discrimination, reasoning, calibration, actionability, novelty and abstention. Dimensions are graded one at a time because a fluent answer pulls every grade up. Zheng and colleagues found that language-model judges show a verbosity bias, rewarding longer answers[17].

    The swap test is the quickest way to find a weak item. Replace the subject with another of the same class. If the question and its expected answer both still hold, the item is too generic. “What does an IC50 measure?” survives any swap and tests background knowledge. “In assay ASY-KIN-07 version 3, how many fold more potent is CPD-0001 than CPD-0005?” dies on swap, because only one table can answer it[data]. The FDA draft asks for credibility within a specific context of use[2]; an item that survives the swap says nothing about that context.

    Difficulty is set by the length of the reasoning chain, not by how obscure the fact is. Looking up a rare value in one table is a basic item. Joining potency to clearance on compound and batch, then noticing that one clearance was measured in a different system, is an advanced one. The awkward cases discriminate best: two runs that disagree, a value with no unit, a result measured at a different condition. Microsomal clearance per mg of protein and hepatocyte clearance per million cells are not interchangeable[18], and an antibody on an immobilised antigen can look much tighter through avidity, the extra grip of binding with two arms at once[19].

    Coverage is planned before any item is written. A facet coverage matrix crosses the facets an item can probe (identity, origin, context, structure, variation, mechanism, methods, results, comparison, hypothesis, risk and negative control) with the knowledge layers. For a leadership team, add a row for each modality class in the portfolio, because each has its own record formats. A small-molecule potency row carries an IC50 in nM with its ATP concentration[data]. A CAR-T (engineered T-cell therapy) release row carries vector copy number per CAR-positive cell and lysis at a stated effector-to-target ratio[data]. A bank that is mostly identity and mechanism questions tests recall, not the work your teams do. This module’s working targets are rules of thumb from this guide’s method, not regulatory requirements. At least three in ten items need more than one source. At least one in ten is a negative control. At least four in ten are advanced or expert. At least eight facets and six question types appear. At least four in ten items are source-grounded. No item fails the swap test. The targets assume a bank of 30 to 40 items; scale them for a smaller bank and state the deviation.

    Negative controls and the hallucination rate

    A hallucination, in this module, is an output that states a value, identifier or citation that no source supports. It is measurable. Walters and Wilder checked 636 citations from 84 short literature reviews written by two versions of one chatbot[20]. They found that 55% of the older version’s citations and 18% of the newer version’s citations were fabricated[20]. Two lessons follow. The rate is real and large enough to matter. And it changed between model versions, so a model update is a change to the thing you validated.

    A negative control is an item whose only correct answer is “no record found”. The SQuAD 2.0 authors showed why a bank needs them. They added over 50,000 unanswerable questions, written to look like answerable ones, to a reading benchmark[21]. A strong system that scored 86% F1 on the original benchmark scored 66% on the new one[21]. F1 is a single score that combines how many of the answers given were right with how many of the right answers were found. A bank without unanswerable items overstates performance, because it never asks the system to stay silent.

    Three kinds of negative control are worth writing. An absent record asks for something that was never recorded: the vector copy number for lot CT-LOT-2024-011, when the release table holds lots CT-LOT-2024-007 to CT-LOT-2024-010 only[data]. Substitution bait asks for something that has a well-known public answer that could be passed off as internal: the response rate in “our” trial STUDY-001, when the extract holds adverse events only[data]. A near-miss asks for a value that exists under a different condition: human liver microsome clearance for CPD-0003, when the only record is rat liver microsomes at 65 µL/min/mg protein[data]. The correct answer to a near-miss names the record that does exist and says why it does not answer the question.

    The headline safety number is the fabrication rate on negative controls: fabricated answers divided by negative-control items. Report it separately from the overall mean, and always with its denominator. Negative controls are a small minority of items, so the rate rests on a small denominator. With 0 fabrications on 3 controls, the exact one-sided 95% upper bound on the true rate is 63% (1 - 0.05^(1/3) = 0.632); with 2 controls it is 78% (1 - 0.05^(1/2) = 0.776). So report the rate with its denominator. With two controls in a 12-item bank, one fabrication moves the rate by 50 points[data]. That is a reason to add controls, not to stop reporting the rate.

    Ground truth with locators: a 12-item mini bank

    Every answer key carries one of four states. EXACT means the answer is stated in the source; the key copies the value, its unit and any qualifier, with a locator (the table, row and column, or the page and section, where the value sits). DERIVED means the answer is computed from stated values; the key shows the formula and a locator for every input. SOURCE-DEPENDENT means the answer needs records outside the supplied source; there is no key, only a validation note saying what a correct answer must contain. ABSENT means the source shows the thing is not there; the key records what was searched. Keys are copied, never written from memory. No identifier goes into a key unless someone read it in the source. Measured values and predicted values are labelled as such. If two sources disagree, the key does not pick one: it states both values with both locators. Mark the items you expect to separate two systems, and report them on their own as well as in the total.

    The rules on test data come from the regulators. The FDA draft says test data should be independent of development data and not used in training[2]. The EMA paper says a hold-out test set is used once, and that a new independent test set is needed after further development[3]. In practice, the bank stays sealed. The vendor never sees the keys, and a bank used to tune the system is retired and replaced.

    Grading without fooling yourself

    Gates are a rule of this guide’s method, not a regulatory requirement. Three failures act as gates: if one fires, the item scores zero whatever else is good. A fabrication gate fires on any invented identifier, citation, sequence or measured value. A false-attribution gate fires when public knowledge is presented as an internal record. A scope gate fires on clinical, dosing, diagnostic or investment advice. Partial credit would misrepresent these failures, because a confident invented value enters someone’s decision. Each fired gate is counted, so the report shows how many there were, not just a lower average.

    Freeze the outputs before grading. The grade must describe what the system said, not a version someone tidied afterwards. The EMA asks for the same discipline at a larger scale: for pivotal trials, pre-processing and models are frozen and documented before unblinding[3]. Graders also check that every cited identifier exists. Walters and Wilder searched several databases to confirm each citation[20]; a plausible lot id that nobody looked up is the failure the whole bank exists to catch.

    A language model can grade at scale. Zheng and colleagues found that strong model judges agreed with human preferences more than 80% of the time, the same level at which humans agree with each other[17]. They also named position bias, verbosity bias and self-enhancement bias, where a model favours answers like its own[17]. So a careful team calibrates. Subject-matter experts (SMEs) grade a sample of items by hand, the team compares their grades with the judge’s, and the rubric is fixed where they disagree. The judge never grades outputs from its own model family, and answer order is shuffled. Gate decisions on fabrication stay with a person.

    Regression items are the ones where a richer configuration did worse than a simpler one, or a new version did worse than the old. Report each item by name. They hide inside an average. In the example run summary, the configuration re-run after a model update fell from 74% to 70% mean score while its fabrications went from one to two, neither of them on a negative control[data]. The four-point drop looks small; the doubled fabrication count is the finding. A leadership review should see the regression list before it sees the averages.

    After go-live: monitoring, change control and the regulator’s file

    A system that passed its bank can drift. The FDA draft notes that changes in input data over time call for life-cycle maintenance of the model[2]. For drug development, the EMA and FDA published ten joint principles of good AI practice in January 2026. They are high-level principles meant to inform future guidance and standards. They include a clear context of use and data governance, with provenance, processing steps and analytical decisions documented traceably[23]. The older 2021 good machine learning practice principles from the FDA, Health Canada and MHRA were written for medical devices; they also include monitoring deployed models and managing the risks of re-training[24]. EU GMP Annex 11, an EU rule for computerised systems used in GMP activities, requires that changes are made only in a controlled way under a defined procedure, and that systems are evaluated periodically[25]. The EU’s draft GMP Annex 22 on AI is a consultation draft, not adopted text. It covers only static models with deterministic output. It says dynamic models, generative AI and large language models should not be used in critical GMP uses[26]. The EU AI Act applies in stages. Most of it applies from 2 August 2026. Obligations for high-risk AI systems start later: 2 December 2027 for the uses listed in its Annex III, and 2 August 2028 for AI in products covered by its Annex I[27].

    Three changes should trigger change control and a re-run of the frozen bank. A model version change is the obvious one. A dictionary version change is easy to miss. MedDRA is the coding dictionary the FDA expects for adverse events in submitted data[28]. It is released twice a year. The March X.0 release can change every level of the hierarchy; the September X.1 release changes only lowest level terms and Preferred Terms[29]. Users should assess the impact of each change on their data output[30]. The example change log treats a MedDRA version change as a change request with its own regression set[data]. A corpus re-index, such as loading new SOP versions into a retrieval index, can silently change answers[data]; the EMA holds the sponsor responsible for models, datasets and pipelines being fit for purpose[3]. The example change log shows the good pattern: the model change CR-2025-016 was re-evaluated on an independent test set and approved by QA and the process owner[data]. The messy version of the same row says “same model family, no re-test”, approved by the data scientist alone[data].

    The file a regulator reads follows the FDA’s seven-step draft: the question of interest, the context of use, the model risk, a credibility plan, executing the plan, documenting results and deviations, and a decision on adequacy for the context of use[2]. In practice the validation report holds the bank version, its composition by layer, type and facet, the keys with locators, the frozen outputs, the gate counts, the fabrication rate on negative controls with its denominator, the judge calibration and every deviation. ICH E6(R3) asks that validation of computerised systems in trials rest on a risk assessment of intended use, the importance of the data and the effect on participants and results[31]. Annex 11 asks for periodic evaluation of computerised systems[25]. In this guide’s method, that review re-runs the bank on a schedule and records the result even when nothing changed.

    Judging a vendor’s evaluation, and the five questions

    A good vendor evaluation runs on your bank, not the vendor’s. It includes a simple baseline: the BEIR benchmark found the keyword method BM25 a robust baseline across 18 datasets, with cheaper dense retrievers often doing worse[32]. It reports negative controls, freezes outputs, names the bank version and labels any vendor-published figure as vendor-published. Responsibility does not move with the work. ICH E6(R3) keeps responsibility for trial conduct and data quality with the sponsor when activities go to service providers[31]. The EMA allows a sponsor to rely on vendor validation documents after assessing them, for example through an audit[33]. Annex 11 asks for formal agreements that state the supplier’s responsibilities[25].

    This frames build, buy or partner without picking a winner. Whichever route you take, the bank, its keys and the SME time behind them are assets you keep. Every candidate faces the same bank, so the comparison is on your records rather than on demos. The five questions from the intermediate module, sharpened for a signing decision[2, 3, 21, 32, 25]:

    1. Which decision, department and system of record? Sharpened: which items in the bank stand for that decision?
    2. Which identifiers join the records, and what share of rows join on every key? Sharpened: does the bank hold items at each join, with keys and locators?
    3. What is the evaluation set? Sharpened: who wrote the answer keys, from which source, has the vendor seen them, and how does the system compare with a simple baseline and its previous version on regression items?
    4. What is the context of use and the cost of a wrong answer? Sharpened: what is the fabrication rate on negative controls, and how many controls were there?
    5. Who keeps it valid when a version changes? Sharpened: who owns monitoring, re-runs of the frozen bank and change control after go-live?

    The evaluation bank extract is the record behind every score. Each item carries the four tags: layer, type, difficulty and facet. In the good table each key has a locator and a frozen output, and the fabricated lot id in ITEM-06 scores zero. In the messy table look for a key with no locator, a key copied wrongly from the source, an output edited before grading, an internal question keyed with a “typical” public value, no abstention item, and a fired fabrication gate that still scored 85[data].

    The run summary compares configurations on one bank. Read fabrications on negative controls first, then all fabrications and abstentions, and the mean score last. In the messy table, look for a missing bank version, a run on fewer items with no controls, a blank fabrication count, and a vendor demo whose outputs were edited before grading.

    The change control log is where governance after go-live becomes visible. Compare the model change and the dictionary change in the two tables: one was re-tested and approved by QA, the other went live on the developer’s word.

    The vendor claim register records what was claimed, against which baseline and by whom. In the messy table look for a speed claim with no metric, a 60x speed figure stripped of its sequence length and hardware, a press release recorded as an independent study, a one-team study labelled as in production, and an adoption figure rewritten as accuracy.

    Try it

    1. Using the good run summary, compute for each configuration the headline rate (fabrications on negative controls divided by negative controls), the fabrication rate over all items, and the abstention accuracy on the controls. Which configuration would you take to a validation decision, and what would you still ask?[data]
      AnswerAll four runs use bank v1.0 with 12 items and 2 negative controls[data]. Headline rate: CFG-A 2 / 2 = 100%; CFG-B 1 / 2 = 50%; CFG-C 0 / 2 = 0%; CFG-D 0 / 2 = 0%. Fabrication rate over all items: CFG-A 4 / 12 = 33.3%; CFG-B 2 / 12 = 16.7%; CFG-C 1 / 12 = 8.3%; CFG-D 2 / 12 = 16.7%. Abstention accuracy on the controls: CFG-A 0 / 2 = 0%; CFG-B 1 / 2 = 50%; CFG-C 2 / 2 = 100%; CFG-D 2 / 2 = 100%. CFG-C is the candidate: no fabrication on a control, the lowest fabrication count overall, no false attributions, both controls abstained, mean 74%. You would still ask which item it fabricated, whether that item touches the context of use, and for more controls, because 2 controls cannot separate 100% from 50% with any confidence.
    2. CFG-D is CFG-C re-run after a model update. Decide whether the update may go live, and say what the change record must show.[data][data]
      AnswerMean score fell from 74% to 70%, and fabrications rose from 1 to 2, so 2 / 12 = 16.7% against 1 / 12 = 8.3% before[data]. The headline rate stayed at 0 / 2, so the new fabrication sits on an answerable item. The update does not go live until that fabrication is found and explained: it is a regression item. The change record should look like the good CR-2025-016 row: risk assessed, performance re-evaluated on an independent test set, tests EVAL-12, approved by QA and the process owner[data]. The messy row (“same model family, no re-test”, approved by the data scientist) is what a careful team never signs[25, 3].
    3. In the messy run summary, CFG-B shows 71% and the vendor demo shows 92%. Explain why neither can be compared with CFG-C’s 74%.[data]
      AnswerCFG-B ran on 10 items with 0 negative controls and blank abstention and control-fabrication counts, so it was never tested for abstention and its denominator differs from 12[data]. The vendor demo’s outputs were edited after the run and before grading, so 92% describes edited text, not the system. This guide’s rule is to freeze outputs before grading[data]. CFG-C’s fabrication count is blank in the messy table. This guide’s rule is that a blank gate count is not zero, so its fabrication rate cannot be reported either; the FDA draft asks that results and deviations be documented[2]. CFG-A has no bank version, so it cannot be placed against the others[2].
    4. Compute the mean score_pct of the good and the messy evaluation bank extracts. Explain the gap item by item.[data]
      AnswerGood: (100 + 100 + 100 + 50 + 100 + 0) / 6 = 450 / 6 = 75.0%. Messy: (100 + 100 + 100 + 90 + 100 + 85) / 6 = 575 / 6 = 95.8%[data]. The 20.8-point gap is made of defects, not performance. ITEM-06 cited a lot id that does not exist, so under this guide’s gate rule it scores 0, not 85. ITEM-04 asks for an internal record but is keyed with a “typical” public value; the key must come from the record (good table: 18 µL/min/mg protein from sm-dmpk), and with no record behind it the item would be SOURCE-DEPENDENT. ITEM-05 was turned from an abstention item into a lookup, so the messy bank has no negative control at all. ITEM-02’s key says 3.4 nM while the source row for CPD-0003 says 3.1 nM[data], so a correct answer would be marked wrong. ITEM-03’s output was not frozen. ITEM-01 has no locator, so nobody can check its key.
    5. Check two keys in the mini bank. Recompute MB-03 from the potency table, taking care with the replicates, and verify it against the pIC50 column. Then say why MB-05 is a near-miss rather than an absent record.[data][data]
      AnswerCPD-0001 has one result, 12.5 nM. CPD-0005 has two replicates, r1 at 55 nM and r2 at 61 nM, all in ASY-KIN-07 v3 at 10 µM ATP[data]. Replicates are summarised as a geometric mean: √(55 × 61) = 57.9 nM, n = 2. 57.9 / 12.5 = 4.6-fold. A key that used r1 alone (4.4-fold) or r2 alone (4.9-fold) would grade a correct answer as wrong. Check: the mean pIC50 of CPD-0005 is (7.26 + 7.21) / 2 = 7.235; 7.9 - 7.235 = 0.665 log units, and 10 to the power 0.665 is about 4.6, so the key holds. MB-05 is a near-miss because a CPD-0003 clearance record does exist, 65 µL/min/mg protein, but in rat liver microsomes[data]. A system that returns 65 as the human value has silently substituted a different species; the correct answer names the rat record and says no human record exists.
    6. Classify the five claims in the good vendor claim register as performance on a task, adoption, or production use, and name the qualifier you must keep for each.[data]
      AnswerVC-001 is task performance: throughput at the same latency against two named systems[data]. VC-002 is task performance from a preprint: 14.2 s for a 384-residue protein on one V100 GPU, 6x faster than one AlphaFold2 model. The preprint’s larger figure of about 60x holds only for short sequences and for the forward pass alone[34]. VC-003 is production use, company-reported, with medical writers overseeing drafts[5]. VC-004 is task performance in a study: 23.3% with a 95% CI of 13.8-32.8%, a width of 32.8 - 13.8 = 19.0 points, in one team of 3 over 4 months, before against after[6]. VC-005 is adoption: more than 70% of staff used the tool voluntarily, which says nothing about accuracy[7]. None of the five replaces a run on your own bank.
    7. Check the 12-item mini bank against this module’s working targets: share of items that need more than one source, share of negative controls, share of advanced plus expert items, number of facets, number of question types, and share of source-grounded items (any layer other than P).
      AnswerMore than one source: MB-04, MB-08, MB-10 and MB-11, 4 / 12 = 33% (target at least 30%). Negative controls: MB-02, MB-05 and MB-12, one of each kind, 3 / 12 = 25% (target at least 10%). Advanced plus expert: MB-04, MB-06, MB-07, MB-08, MB-10 and MB-11, 6 / 12 = 50% (target at least 40%). Facets: 9 of 12 (structure, negative control, results, context, comparison, variation, risk, identity, methods; target at least 8). Question types: 7 (factual, abstention, quantitative, integration, comparative, troubleshooting, retrieval; target at least 6). Source-grounded: every item except MB-01, 11 / 12 = 92% (target at least 40%). The bank meets every target. Its gap is size: 3 controls is still too few to estimate a fabrication rate with any confidence.
    8. Build a value case. VC-004 in the claim register is a 23.3% time saving (95% CI 13.8 to 32.8%) in one team of 3[6]. Suppose, as an illustration, your team spends 400 hours a year on the same task. Give a planning range, the counterfactual and a kill criterion.[data]
      Answer400 × 0.138 = 55.2 hours and 400 × 0.328 = 131.2 hours a year, so plan on about 55 to 131 hours, with 400 × 0.233 = 93.2 hours as the point estimate. The range comes from one small before-and-after study with no separate control group, so it is a planning range, not a forecast[6]. The counterfactual is the same team doing the same task over the same period without the tool; a before-and-after design cannot rule out other changes over that time. Adoption (who used the tool) is not the outcome (hours saved at equal quality, checked on your own bank). A kill criterion, set before go-live as a rule of this guide: stop and review if the measured saving after 3 months is below 55.2 / 4 = 13.8 hours, the low end of the range pro rata, or if any fabrication gate fires on a negative control.

    Check yourself

    1. A system passes most P items and fails most S items. The most likely cause is: a) the model lacks knowledge b) retrieval is broken c) the judge is biased d) the bank is too easy
    2. Asked for human microsomal clearance of CPD-0003, where only a rat record exists, the item is: a) an absent record b) substitution bait c) a near-miss d) a regression item
    3. Which ground-truth state carries no key, only a validation note? a) EXACT b) DERIVED c) SOURCE-DEPENDENT d) ABSENT
    4. An excellent answer cites a lot id that does not exist. It scores: a) partial credit b) full credit c) zero, counted toward the hallucination rate d) zero, not counted
    5. The vendor updates the model inside a validated system. You should: a) accept it, same model family b) raise change control and re-run the frozen bank, reviewing regression items c) wait for the next periodic review d) accept the vendor’s demo results

    Answer key: 1-b, 2-c, 3-c, 4-c, 5-b
