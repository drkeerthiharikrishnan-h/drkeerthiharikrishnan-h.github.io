Keerthi's Notes

Issue 04 · 16 – 22 November 2026 · 4 min read
Keerthi Harikrishnan · developmental and cancer biologist, building AI for science

The thread through this week’s reading: checking. Noise in drug-response assays, bias in knowledge graphs, and errors in the published record. Four papers, three news pieces, the week’s deals, and one health hackathon open until 4 December.

Papers

bioRxivPreprint
bioRxiv · Preprint

Drug-response models that allow for noise in the assay

Models trained on cancer cell line drug screens usually treat the viability readout as exact. The authors show measurement noise in four widely used screening datasets. A model that ignores it can learn experimental artefacts instead of biology. Their framework, DeepCINET, uses a ranking approach that accounts for the noise. They report better generalisation across experimental conditions and to unseen samples. The gains were clear in picking out the most drug-sensitive cell lines.

bioRxivPreprint
bioRxiv · Preprint

Safety predictions for targets that few have studied

Knowledge graphs are used to predict links between a drug target and adverse events. A few highly connected “hub” nodes carry most of the signal, so predictions can be poor for new or understudied targets, where they are needed most. The authors normalise graph-derived features (metapaths) by node degree, a method they call Inverse Node Frequency. They report a higher true positive rate for low-degree targets than with the untransformed features or an established normalisation method. They also tested it on an external set of associations not used for modelling.

bioRxivPreprint
bioRxiv · Preprint

Testing interpretability claims against curated protein annotations

Claims about what a network has learned are rarely tested against ground truth that does not come from the model. Proteins offer one: curators annotate functional sites residue by residue. The authors tested three ESMC protein language models (300 million to 6 billion parameters). The answer to what a model has learned changed with the layer read, the decoding method, the units picked and the score used. When they shuffled the annotations and skipped held-out validation, almost every latent unit still got a label. Their advice: check a model against the annotations themselves.

Nature MethodsPerspective
Nature Methods · Perspective · image: Nature Methods

Two platforms to model human brain development

No one model system covers the whole of human brain development, from the first neural tissue to mature circuits. Organoids cannot fully reproduce neural induction, and do not reach the circuit maturation that needs a living body. The authors propose two platforms: stem cell-based embryo models for the early steps, and interspecies chimeras for maturation. They set out reporting standards, including benchmarks for neural-tube formation and closure, and an oversight framework tied to developmental landmarks.

News and blogs

NatureNews
Nature · News · image: Nature

More than 220 papers show proteins the cells could not have made

A scientific sleuth found more than 220 papers in which a protein appears in imaging experiments, although the genetically altered cell line lacks the gene to make it. Journals are retracting or correcting the work. Researchers warn there are probably many more.

NatureNews
Nature · News · image: Nature

US academies: journals take too long to fix errors

A report from the US National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine says publishers are too slow to retract or correct papers, and apply standards unevenly. Retractions come about 2.4 years after publication on average, according to the Retraction Watch database. The report recommends that editorial review finish within 12 weeks in most cases and rarely take more than 6 months, for concerns about a single paper. It also says journals should disclose when they were alerted to a problem.

GENNews
GEN · News · image: GEN

A mouse model of X-linked dystonia-parkinsonism, built by genome writing

An NYU Langone Health-led team replaced part of the mouse Taf1 gene with human DNA. The 78-kilobase stretch of TAF1 came from a patient and carries the SVA retrotransposon insertion that causes the disease. SVAs occur in humans but not in mice, so standard mouse models do not show what they do. When the human allele was expressed in the nervous system, the striatum wasted away. That is the region damaged in people with XDP. Disease that takes decades to develop in people appeared in the mice within two months. The study is published in Cell.
