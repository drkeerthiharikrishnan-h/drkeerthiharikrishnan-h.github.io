Issue 03 · 9 – 15 November 2026 · 3 min read
Keerthi Harikrishnan · developmental and cancer biologist, building AI for science

The thread through this week’s reading: large reference datasets, and plain tests of the models that use them. Four papers, three news pieces and the week’s deals. The only health event in the feed, a student hackathon (Amador Valley Girls Who Code, 6–16 November), closed the day this issue went out.

Papers

bioRxivPreprint
bioRxiv · Preprint

Folding confidence gives a useful but limited signal of antibody binding

Antibody design often treats high confidence from a structure-prediction model as a sign of real binding. Earlier tests of that idea used small sets with few targets. The authors used a large language model to extract 89,074 antibody-antigen records from 10,110 US patents (POISK). From these they built a balanced test of 22,494 complexes for six structure-prediction models. The best, Protenix-v2, told binder from decoy in 72% of pairs and found the true binder among 50 candidates in 29% of trials. The authors suggest folding models should be paired with other methods.

bioRxiv · Preprint

A drug-repurposing model that lost to its baselines, and said so

PRECISION, a graph neural network for triple-negative breast cancer, describes cell lines mostly by transcription factor activity. On held-out cell lines it scored below two references: the average response per drug, and a random forest on transcription factor activities. The abstract states this plainly. Simpler per-drug models linked predicted sensitivity to survival in two breast cancer cohorts. But the seven drugs a selection rule picked were not supported in TNBC cell lines or a triple-negative cohort. The authors call the results hypotheses, not validated treatments.

bioRxivPreprint
bioRxiv · Preprint

From spots to single cells in spatial transcriptomics

Each spot in spatial transcriptomics holds signal from several cells. Recovering single-cell profiles needs two steps: find the individual cells, then estimate each gene’s expression in each one. st2sc finds candidate cells from a transformer’s attention maps, then predicts each cell’s gene counts. The authors report it did better than earlier methods on synthetic and Visium-like data, and applied it to human lung with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

bioRxivPreprint
bioRxiv · Preprint

Which phosphosites matter, not only where they are

Finding modification sites on proteins and predicting which phosphosites change function are usually modelled apart. HieraPTM finds the sites; a second module, HieraFuncPhos, reuses what it learned and adds evidence from protein mechanics to rank functional phosphosites (AUROC 0.8274). At two sites on HSP90AA1, its mechanical patterns agreed with published NMR observations.

News and blogs

GENNews
GEN · News · image: GEN

A cell atlas of early breast cancer

MD Anderson researchers mapped the tissue around oestrogen-receptor-positive ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), the most common stage 0 breast cancer. They compared normal breast, DCIS and invasive cancer. Features linked to invasive disease appear earlier than previously known. Not every DCIS progresses; the atlas may help predict which will.

NatureNews
Nature · News · image: Nature

A 25.5-million-cell atlas of the basal ganglia

25.5 million cells from humans, macaques and marmosets, described in 26 papers in Cell, Neuron and four other journals. The basal ganglia are linked to Parkinson’s and Huntington’s disease, schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and addiction.

NatureNews
Nature · News · image: Nature

A CRISPR therapy given inside the body nears FDA approval

Intellia’s lonvo-z uses CRISPR–Cas9 to treat a form of hereditary angioedema. The developer reports it still works four years after treatment. Current gene-editing treatments require removing a patient’s cells; this one edits genes inside the body.
